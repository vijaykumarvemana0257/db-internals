Two workloads written in the same language want opposite machines. One fetches a customer
and their last three orders, twenty thousand times a second, and touches maybe forty rows
per request. The other reads two columns out of two hundred, across nine billion rows, and
is allowed to take a minute. Every structural decision in the rest of this site — page
layout, index choice, compression, concurrency control, replication topology — follows from
which of those two you are building for, and the single number that separates them is
**bytes read per answer**.

## The two shapes, measured

"OLTP" and "OLAP" are unhelpful as acronyms and precise as workload descriptions. The
distinction is not *transactional versus analytical intent*; it is the shape of the access.

| | OLTP | OLAP |
|---|---|---|
| Rows touched per query | 1 to a few hundred, found by index | 10⁶–10¹² , found by scan |
| Columns touched | most of the row (`SELECT *` is normal) | 2–10 of 50–500 |
| Latency budget | sub-millisecond to ~10 ms | seconds to minutes |
| Concurrency | thousands of short statements | tens of long ones |
| Writes | many small, in place, durable per commit | bulk append, rewrite by partition |
| The expensive resource | random I/O and lock/latch contention | sequential bandwidth and CPU per value |
| Working set | the hot tail, ideally resident in the buffer pool | everything, never resident |

A row store optimizes the first column of that table. It stores whole tuples contiguously in
a page — the **n-ary storage model**, NSM — so one 8 KB read yields every column of every row
on that page. When you want the whole row, that is exactly right: one random I/O gives you
the answer. When you want two of two hundred columns, you have still paid for all two
hundred; the other 198 rode along in the same cache lines and were discarded.

A column store optimizes the second. It splits the relation vertically, storing each
attribute's values together — the **decomposition storage model**, DSM, named by Copeland and
Khoshafian in 1985 and made practical by C-Store and MonetDB/X100 twenty years later. A query
reads only the column files it references, so bytes read scale with *columns touched*, not
row width.

## Why columns compress and rows don't

The second effect is larger than the first and less obvious. A column is a run of values of
one type, from one domain, usually correlated with its neighbours — a hundred thousand
`status` values drawn from six strings, timestamps that increase by a few seconds at a time,
prices clustered in a narrow band. That is ideal input for dictionary encoding, run-length
encoding, delta and frame-of-reference encoding, then a general compressor on top (ClickHouse
defaults to LZ4 per column and will use ZSTD on request). A row is a struct of unrelated
types; an int next to a UUID next to a timestamp compresses like noise.

So the bytes-read ratio between the two layouts is roughly

```
row store bytes     rows × row width
─────────────────  ≈  ───────────────────────────────────────────
column store bytes  rows × columns touched × column width ÷ CR
```

with a compression ratio `CR` of 3–10× on real analytic data. Forty columns of 24 bytes,
three of them touched, compressing 4×: the column store reads roughly 53× fewer bytes for the
same answer. Drag **columns touched** in the lab from 3 to 40 and that 53× collapses to 4× —
the compression ratio, all that is left once you are reading every column anyway — exactly as
the formula predicts.

Three more structures ride on the columnar layout and matter as much as the encoding:

- **Zone maps.** Every block carries min/max (and often null count) per column, so a
  predicate can skip blocks without decompressing them. ClickHouse's MergeTree keeps one
  sparse index mark per **granule of 8192 rows**; Parquet keeps statistics per row group
  (commonly 128 MB) and per page (~1 MB); Snowflake prunes **micro-partitions** of 50–500 MB
  uncompressed on the same principle. Pruning is only as good as the correlation between the
  predicate and the physical order, which is why sort keys, clustering keys and partition keys
  are the tuning surface of every warehouse.
- **Vectorized execution.** Operators process batches of values rather than one tuple at a
  time — DuckDB's standard vector is 2048 values — so per-tuple interpreter overhead amortizes
  and the inner loops become SIMD-friendly. Per-value CPU falls from tens of nanoseconds to
  low single digits.
- **Late materialization.** Filters run on the encoded column, producing a selection vector;
  the remaining columns are only fetched for surviving positions.

The price is symmetric and unavoidable: a column store cannot update a row in place. One row's
values live in as many places as there are columns, all compressed inside immutable blocks.
ClickHouse implements `ALTER TABLE … UPDATE` as a *mutation* that rewrites whole parts in the
background; SQL Server's clustered columnstore takes inserts into a row-oriented **delta
rowgroup** and only compresses it once it reaches about 1 048 576 rows. That is why the write
half of the lab flips so hard when you leave the append shape: single-row updates on a column
store cost hundreds of microseconds of part rewriting against tens of microseconds of B+tree
maintenance.

## The crossovers you can watch invert

Two crossovers show up in the lab, and both of them show up in real query plans.

**Index scan versus sequential scan, inside one row store.** An index plan costs a tree
descent plus one random heap fetch per matching row; a sequential scan costs the whole table
at streaming bandwidth. The index wins while matches are few and loses once they aren't,
because `matched × random_page_cost` eventually exceeds `table_pages × seq_page_cost` — with
PostgreSQL's defaults of 4.0 and 1.0, the switch happens at single-digit percent selectivity,
and a bitmap heap scan occupies the middle ground by sorting the TIDs so the heap is read in
physical order. Slide selectivity across that point in the lab and the row store's own plan
label changes under you.

**Row store versus column store, across engines.** Push selectivity down far enough and the
row store's index plan beats everything, because it reads one page per answer while the column
store still has to open a granule. Push it up and the column store wins by two to three orders
of magnitude. The lab prints where the lead changes hands for your current settings; that
sentence is the entire OLTP/OLAP boundary, and it moves when you change row width, columns
touched or table size.

:::caution[The numbers in the lab are a model, not a benchmark]
Every term is real arithmetic over stated constants — one cold random 8 KB read at 25 µs, a
2 GB/s streaming scan, 2 ns per value in a vectorized loop — but there is no buffer-pool
warmth, no I/O concurrency and no parallelism in it. Real systems close some of these gaps
with caching and many cores. The *ordering* and the *crossover positions* are the lesson; the
absolute times are not.
:::

## The specialized families

Beyond the two general shapes, several structures exist because a specific access pattern has
an index that a B+tree cannot provide.

| Family | Structure | Built for | What it cannot do |
|---|---|---|---|
| Time-series — Prometheus, InfluxDB, TimescaleDB | Time-partitioned chunks, per-series columnar blocks, delta and XOR encoding (Prometheus cuts head chunks at 120 samples) | Append-only numeric streams; queries over a recent time window with high compression | Arbitrary joins; updating old points means decompressing and rewriting a chunk |
| Search — Elasticsearch, OpenSearch, Lucene, Postgres GIN | Inverted index: term → posting list of doc ids, plus BKD trees for numeric ranges and columnar **doc values** for sorting and aggregation | Matching text against a query — Postgres spells it `to_tsvector(body) @@ to_tsquery('…')` — plus ranking and faceting | Transactions; read-your-writes (Elasticsearch's default `index.refresh_interval` is 1 s, so a write is invisible to search until the next refresh); cheap updates — a document change is a delete plus a full reindex of every token |
| Vector — pgvector, Milvus, Qdrant | HNSW proximity graph (pgvector defaults: `m` 16, `ef_construction` 64, `hnsw.ef_search` 40) or IVF lists | Nearest-neighbour search over embeddings in logarithmic-ish hops instead of a full distance scan | Exactness — results are *approximate*, recall is a tuning knob; an insert costs a graph search |
| Streaming log — Kafka, Redpanda, Pulsar | Append-only partitioned segments, addressed by offset, read with zero-copy sequential I/O | Ordered durable hand-off and replay; fan-out to many independent consumers | Any query at all — no secondary index, no aggregation, no point lookup by key |
| Cache — Redis, Memcached | In-memory hash table; Redis executes commands on a single thread, Memcached on many | Point reads at network-RTT latency, ahead of any disk-backed engine | Scans and secondary access paths (`SCAN` walks the keyspace); durability is opt-in and bounded |
| Embedded — SQLite, DuckDB, RocksDB, LMDB | The engine is a library in your process; no server, no network | Zero-latency local access; SQLite for OLTP, DuckDB for OLAP over local Parquet | Multi-writer concurrency across machines — SQLite allows one writer at a time |

Switch the lab's query shape to **Full text** or **Similarity** and the point is stark: the
row store and the column store are not slightly worse, they are four to six orders of
magnitude worse, because they have no structure for the predicate and must evaluate it on
every row. That gap — not vendor preference — is what justifies operating a second system.

## HTAP, and what it actually costs

HTAP ("hybrid transactional/analytical processing", a Gartner coinage from 2014) is the
attempt to serve both shapes from one system by maintaining both physical layouts:

- **TiDB** keeps the row format in TiKV and adds **TiFlash** columnar replicas that join the
  Raft group as *learners*, applying the same replicated log. The optimizer costs both and
  routes per query; analytical reads land on replicas that transactions never touch.
- **SingleStore** unifies rowstore and columnstore in one table type, with recent rows in a
  row-oriented segment and older ones compressed columnar behind a seekable index.
- **Oracle Database In-Memory** is dual-format: the same table exists as row blocks on disk
  and as an **In-Memory Column Store** in the SGA, populated from those blocks. The column copy
  is not persisted and is not another durability path — it is a cache with a different layout.
- **SQL Server** offers columnstore indexes over row tables, including nonclustered
  columnstore for exactly this hybrid case; **MySQL HeatWave** attaches an in-memory column
  engine to InnoDB.

All of them pay in the same three places, and you can see the first in the lab. Writes get
more expensive because every row must be applied to both layouts and, in the distributed
cases, replicated to extra nodes. Resource isolation becomes the operational problem: a
runaway analytical query still competes for CPU, memory and network with the transactions
that fund the business, and the isolation mechanisms are scheduler knobs rather than physics.
And freshness is a dial, not a guarantee — the columnar side is some distance behind, whether
that distance is a Raft learner's apply lag or a delta rowgroup waiting to compress.

HTAP is a real answer for a real class of problems: operational analytics on fresh data,
where a five-minute-old copy would be useless and the analytical volume is moderate. It is not
the general answer, because it makes the hardest thing about the architecture — isolation
between a workload with a 5 ms budget and one with a 5 minute budget — an internal concern of
a single process.

## The deployment nearly everyone converges on

The usual answer is not one system. It is **one system of record plus derived stores**, and
the connective tissue is the write-ahead log you already have.

The system of record is a row store: PostgreSQL, InnoDB, or a distributed equivalent. It owns
the constraints, the transactions and the only writable copy of the truth. Its log is then
read by change data capture — PostgreSQL logical decoding through a replication slot with the
`pgoutput` plugin, MySQL's row-based binlog, usually via Debezium — and published, typically
into Kafka. Every other store subscribes: the column store for analytics, the search index for
text, the vector index for embeddings, the cache for hot reads. The strip under the lab's
ranking shows where the winning family sits in that picture.

The properties that make this the default are worth stating explicitly:

- **Every derived store is rebuildable.** Drop it, replay the log or re-snapshot the source,
  and it comes back. That means schema changes, engine upgrades and migrations are routine
  rather than terrifying, because nothing unique lives there.
- **There is exactly one writer of truth**, so constraints and transactions have a single
  place to be enforced. The alternative — the application writing to the database *and* the
  search index *and* the cache, so-called dual writes — has no atomicity across the two and
  drifts permanently the first time one call fails. The **transactional outbox** (write the
  event to a table in the same transaction, publish from the log) is the standard repair.
- **Each store is chosen per access shape**, which is the lab's whole argument turned into an
  architecture.
- **The cost is a consistency window.** Derived stores are eventually consistent, delivery is
  at-least-once (so consumers must be idempotent), and ordering is per partition, not global.
  Read-your-own-writes for a freshly indexed document is something you engineer, not something
  you get.

## Where it bites in production

- **Reporting queries on the OLTP primary.** A 40-minute analytical query holds an open
  snapshot, which pins the `xmin` horizon, which stops autovacuum from reclaiming *any* dead
  tuple newer than it. Tables bloat, index scans slow down, and the trigger is invisible in
  query latency until it isn't. Watch `backend_xmin` in `pg_stat_activity` and the
  `oldest_xmin` reported by vacuum; InnoDB's equivalent symptom is a growing history list
  length in `SHOW ENGINE INNODB STATUS`.
- **Moving those queries to a replica moves the problem.** With `hot_standby_feedback = on`
  the replica's snapshot pins the primary's horizon anyway; with it off, long queries die with
  `canceling statement due to conflict with recovery` once `max_standby_streaming_delay` is
  exceeded. Pick which of the two failures you want.
- **A replication slot with no consumer fills the primary's disk.** A slot guarantees the WAL
  is retained until the subscriber acknowledges it. Stop a CDC connector on a Friday and you
  can be woken by `pg_wal` exhausting the volume. `max_slot_wal_keep_size` bounds it at the
  cost of invalidating the slot — and re-seeding the derived store.
- **Row-at-a-time inserts into a column store.** Every small insert creates a part or
  rowgroup; merges cannot keep up, and ClickHouse eventually refuses writes outright with
  *Too many parts* once a partition exceeds `parts_to_throw_insert`. The fix is batching —
  thousands of rows per insert, or asynchronous inserts that batch server-side.
- **A search index or cache used as the system of record.** No transactions, no constraints,
  no foreign keys, a refresh delay between write and visibility, and a mapping you cannot
  change without a full reindex. The day it is the only copy of something is the day you
  learn which of those matters most.
- **The "just add an index" reflex on an analytical query.** A report touching six columns
  over the whole table cannot be fixed with a B+tree; it needs a different layout. Index
  sprawl instead adds `1 + secondary_indexes` random write amplification to every insert on
  the hot path — the lab's writes-per-row number — to speed up nothing.

:::note[What to take forward]
The workload shape decides the engine, and the shape is captured by two numbers: how many rows
an answer touches, and how much of each row it needs. Few rows and all columns wants a row
store with a B+tree; all rows and few columns wants a compressed columnar layout with zone
maps and vectorized execution; predicates a B+tree cannot answer — text, similarity, ordered
streams — want their own index family. HTAP buys freshness by maintaining both layouts and
charges you in write cost and isolation; the common alternative is one system of record whose
log feeds derived stores you can always throw away and rebuild.
:::
