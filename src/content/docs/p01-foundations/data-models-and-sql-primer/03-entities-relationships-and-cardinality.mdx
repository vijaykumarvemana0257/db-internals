A schema is a set of claims about what can exist. Most of those claims are not about types —
they are about *counts*: whether an order can exist without a customer, whether a customer can
have two, whether a student and a course can be paired more than once. Get a count wrong and
you do not get a bug, you get a table that cannot hold next quarter's reality, and a migration
that touches every query in the codebase.

## Three nouns, and what each one really is

An **entity type** is a set of things you want to record facts about: `Customer`, `Order`,
`Course`. An **entity instance** is one member of that set. The distinction matters because
almost every diagram is drawn at the type level and almost every argument about it is really
about instances — "can a customer have two addresses?" is a question about instances that only
the type-level box can answer.

An **attribute** is a function from an entity instance to a value in a domain: `Customer →
email`. The words "a function" carry real weight. A function returns exactly one value, so an
attribute that can return two — three phone numbers, a set of tags — is not an attribute of
that entity. It is another entity, or at minimum another table. This is the single most
productive test in conceptual modeling and the rest of this page keeps returning to it.

A **relationship type** is a subset of the Cartesian product of two (or more) entity sets: a
relationship instance is a tuple of entity instances that belong together. `places ⊆ Customer ×
Order`. Every question this page asks — cardinality, participation — is a question about which
subsets are legal.

That definition already tells you something the diagrams do not. A relationship *set* has no
duplicates, because sets do not. If a student can enrol in the same course twice in different
terms, then "enrols" is not a relationship between `Student` and `Course`; the term is part of
the identity, and you are looking at an `Enrolment` entity with its own key. Relationships that
grow attributes — a grade, an enrolment date, a role — are entities wearing a disguise.

## Cardinality is the maximum

Cardinality answers: at most how many instances of B may one instance of A be related to, and
vice versa. The three named shapes come from the two maximums:

- **1:1** — each A relates to at most one B and each B to at most one A. `Person` holds at most
  one current `Passport`; a `Passport` belongs to one `Person`.
- **1:N** — one A may relate to many B, but each B to at most one A. One `Customer`, many
  `Order`s. This is the overwhelming majority of edges in a real schema.
- **M:N** — many on both sides. `Student` and `Course`; `Post` and `Tag`.

The reason this is the first thing you decide is that the three shapes have *different physical
representations*, and only one of them is cheap to change:

| Shape | Representation | What changing it costs later |
|---|---|---|
| 1:N | A foreign key column on the many side | Nothing — it is already the general case within "at most one parent" |
| 1:1 | A foreign key on one side with a `UNIQUE` constraint | Dropping the `UNIQUE` turns it into 1:N in place |
| M:N | A third table whose primary key is both foreign keys | A new table, a data migration, and every query that touched the column |

:::tip[A 1:1 is often two tables that should be one]
If both ends are total — every `Person` has exactly one `Passport` and vice versa — the two
entities have the same key and the same lifetime, and the honest representation is usually one
table. Splitting is justified by access, not by the diagram: a rarely-read column that is
megabytes wide, or a column with a different security boundary. PostgreSQL weakens even that
argument, because TOAST already does the split for you: once a row exceeds about 2 KB (a quarter
of the 8 KB page), the widest variable-length values are compressed and, if that is still not
enough, moved out to a side table, and an out-of-line value is not read at all unless the query
selects that column.
:::

A foreign key column cannot express M:N — a column holds one value, so it can point at one
parent. That is the entire reason junction tables exist, and the reason an M:N relationship
discovered late is expensive: the column you were storing the parent in has to become rows in a
new table, and every `JOIN`, `INSERT` and report that read it has to be rewritten.

:::caution[Two meanings of "cardinality"]
In modeling, cardinality is the *maximum count on a relationship edge* — the subject of this
page. In query optimization, cardinality is the *estimated number of rows* a plan node produces,
and "cardinality estimation" is the part of the optimizer that gets blamed when a plan goes
wrong. Same word, unrelated quantity; the context always makes it clear which one is meant, but
the collision catches people once.
:::

## Participation is the minimum

Cardinality alone leaves the more common production question unanswered: is the relationship
*required*? That is **participation**, and it has two values per end:

- **Total** (mandatory) — every instance of that entity must participate in at least one
  relationship instance. Every `Order` has a `Customer`.
- **Partial** (optional) — an instance may participate in none. A `Customer` may have no
  `Order`s yet.

Cross the three cardinalities with total and partial and you get the six shapes the textbooks
name — the matrix in the lab. Because each end carries its own minimum, a fully specified edge is
one of twelve combinations, and each one lands somewhere different in DDL:

| Shape | DDL |
|---|---|
| 1:N, total on the many side — every `Order` has a `Customer` | `customer_id int NOT NULL REFERENCES customer` on `order` |
| 1:N, partial on the many side — an `Order` may have none | the same column, nullable; orphan orders are legal |
| 1:N, total on the one side — every `Customer` has an `Order` | **nothing declarative.** See below |
| 1:1, total on the side holding the FK | FK column with `UNIQUE NOT NULL` |
| 1:1, partial both ways | FK column with `UNIQUE`, nullable |
| M:N | junction table with `PRIMARY KEY (student_id, course_id)` |

That last row is the one worth internalizing. "Every `Order` must have at least one
`OrderLine`" is total participation on `Order`, and `Order` holds no foreign key — the FK is on
`order_line`. There is no column to mark `NOT NULL`. A declarative foreign key can enforce
"this child has a parent"; it can never enforce "this parent has a child".

SQL-92 specified `CREATE ASSERTION` for exactly this — a standalone, multi-table predicate the
database checks at commit — and no mainstream engine implements it. What you actually have:

- **PostgreSQL:** `CREATE CONSTRAINT TRIGGER ... DEFERRABLE INITIALLY DEFERRED`, which fires at
  commit rather than at statement time, so an order and its first line inserted in one
  transaction are both visible when the check runs.
- **Everywhere:** insert parent and child in the same transaction and check in the application,
  which is what most teams do, which is why most large schemas contain some rows that violate a
  total participation the diagram claims.

If you have ever run `SELECT count(*) FROM "order" o WHERE NOT EXISTS (SELECT 1 FROM order_line
l WHERE l.order_id = o.id)` on a mature database and been surprised, this is why.

## Reading the two notations

The lab draws the same four numbers twice. Both notations put the **maximums** beside the same
entity, and they read *across*: the number or symbol next to `Order` tells you how many
`Order`s one `Customer` may have. Chen writes it as a ratio label — `1` next to `Customer`, `N`
next to `Order`; UML writes a multiplicity, `0..*`, at the same end; crow's foot draws a three-
pronged foot touching the entity when the maximum is many and a single bar when it is one.

**Participation is where the two notations disagree about position**, and it trips up everyone
who moves between them. In Chen, total participation of `Customer` is a *double line between
`Customer` and the relationship diamond* — drawn on the `Customer` end. In crow's foot, the
same constraint is the *inner symbol at the `Order` end*: a second bar for mandatory, a circle
for optional. Same claim, opposite ends of the line. Toggle a minimum in the lab and watch the
double line and the bar move to opposite sides of the diagram together.

The four crow's-foot ends, outer symbol first (the one touching the entity):

| Outer symbol (touching the entity) | Inner symbol | Reads as | Min | Max |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| bar | bar | exactly one | 1 | 1 |
| bar | circle | zero or one | 0 | 1 |
| crow's foot | bar | one or more | 1 | many |
| crow's foot | circle | zero or more | 0 | many |

:::caution[Look-here vs look-across]
Chen ratio labels, UML multiplicities and crow's-foot symbols are all **look-across**: the
annotation beside an entity counts *that* entity, per one instance of the other. The
`(min,max)` notation from the Merise tradition — still standard in the French-language
literature and in some tooling — is **look-here**: the pair beside `Customer` describes how many
relationships one `Customer` participates in. The two conventions put `(0,N)` and `(1,1)` on
opposite ends of the same edge. Before arguing about a diagram, establish which one it uses.
:::

## Weak entities and identifying relationships

Some entities have no key of their own. An `OrderLine` is "line 2" — of *which* order? The
number alone identifies nothing. Such an entity is **weak**, or existence-dependent: its
identity is borrowed from an owner, and the relationship that supplies it is **identifying**.

Three things follow, and they are consequences, not conventions:

1. The weak entity's key is the owner's key plus a local **discriminator** (also called a
   partial key): `PRIMARY KEY (order_id, line_no)`.
2. Participation on the weak side is necessarily total and the maximum is necessarily one — a
   weak entity cannot exist without exactly one owner. Flip "identifying" on in the lab and the
   owner end pins itself to `1..1`; that is the invariant, not a UI choice.
3. Deletion cascades are the default reading. `ON DELETE CASCADE` is *correct* for an
   identifying relationship, because the child has no independent existence, and usually wrong
   for a non-identifying one.

Chen draws a weak entity as a double rectangle and the identifying relationship as a double
diamond. IDEF1X is a separate standard, and the one ERwin-style tools mix into their
crow's-foot diagrams: it draws the dependent entity with rounded corners and the identifying
relationship as a *solid* line, reserving the dashed line for non-identifying. Both are in the
lab.

The composite key is not just bookkeeping. In InnoDB the table *is* its primary-key B+tree, so
`PRIMARY KEY (order_id, line_no)` physically stores one order's lines contiguously, and fetching
them is a single short range scan. With a surrogate `line_id` primary key and a secondary index
on `order_id`, the same fetch becomes an index scan plus one random primary-key lookup per row.
PostgreSQL stores the heap separately and gets no such clustering for free — but the composite
key still expresses the truth, which is that `line_no` means nothing outside its order.

## The attributes that are really entities

Two attribute kinds from the ER vocabulary exist precisely to flag things that should not be
columns:

**Multi-valued attributes** (Chen: a double ellipse) — a customer's phone numbers, a post's
tags. An attribute is a function returning one value; if it can return a set, it is a
relationship to another entity. In a schema this shows up in two smells:

- *The repeated-column smell:* `phone1`, `phone2`, `phone3`. It survives until the fourth phone
  number arrives, and then you pay with a schema change plus every query that enumerated the
  columns. Worse, "does this customer have number X?" has to be written `WHERE phone1 = $1 OR
  phone2 = $1 OR phone3 = $1`, which no single index serves well.
- *The comma-separated-list smell:* `tags text` holding `'sql,indexes,btree'`. Every lookup
  becomes `WHERE tags LIKE '%btree%'`, which a B-tree index cannot serve at all — a leading
  wildcard has no prefix to descend on — so it degrades to a full scan. And `'btree'` matches
  `'nonbtree'`.

The legitimate exceptions are typed and indexable, not string soup: PostgreSQL's `text[]` or
`jsonb` with a GIN index answers `tags @> ARRAY['btree']` without a scan. That is a
denormalization with a named cost (no referential integrity to a tag table, no per-tag
attributes), not a free pass.

**Derived attributes** (Chen: a dashed ellipse) — `total_spent`, `age`, `order_total`. They are
computable from other data, so storing them is a caching decision with a consistency bill. Store
one and you own keeping it true: a trigger, a materialized view, or a batch job, plus the
reconciliation query for when it drifts. Model it as derived first and decide to store it second,
deliberately.

The three questions that turn an attribute into an entity: *Can there ever be more than one?*
*Does it have attributes of its own?* (an enrolment has a grade; a "tag" has a display name) and
*Will anything ever query by it, or join to it?* Any yes means it wants its own table.

## Where it bites in production

The cardinality mistakes that cost the most are not exotic:

- **An M:N that was modeled as 1:N.** A `product.category_id` that has to become `product_category`
  rows. New table, backfill, and every read path rewritten — the most expensive routine migration
  in application development.
- **A 1:N joined for aggregation.** `SELECT o.id, sum(l.amount), o.shipping FROM "order" o JOIN
  order_line l ...` — the join fans out one order row into N, and any aggregate over an
  order-level column now double-counts. The fix is aggregating in a subquery, but the bug is a
  cardinality misreading.
- **Optional participation you did not want.** A nullable FK means every consumer must handle
  `NULL`, every join becomes a `LEFT JOIN` to avoid silently dropping rows, and the "impossible"
  orphan appears the first time a delete races an insert. If participation is total, say so with
  `NOT NULL` at creation time; adding it later means finding and fixing the rows that already
  violate it.
- **Total participation nobody could enforce.** The parent-must-have-a-child case above. Know
  which of your diagram's constraints the database is actually enforcing and which are
  aspirations enforced by code review.

:::note[What to take forward]
A relationship edge is four numbers: a minimum and a maximum at each end. The maximums give the
three cardinalities and decide the physical shape — FK column, FK plus `UNIQUE`, or a junction
table. The minimums give participation, which becomes `NOT NULL` when the end holding the FK is
mandatory and becomes nothing at all when it is the other end. An attribute that can hold two
values, or that has attributes of its own, is an entity that has not been promoted yet. The
[next page](../../../p01-foundations/data-models-and-sql-primer/04-mapping-an-er-model-to-tables/) turns
each of these shapes into DDL.
:::
