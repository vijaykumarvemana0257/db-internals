Both kinds of drive lie to you, and they lie in different ways. A hard disk pretends to be a
flat array of sectors while charging you for arm movement and rotation you cannot see. A
solid-state drive pretends to be the same flat array while running a log-structured
filesystem in firmware, quietly copying your data around to make room. Page size, sequential
logs, LSM compaction and where you put `fsync` are all answers to one of those two lies.

## The mechanical drive: seek, rotate, transfer

<PlatterSeekScheduler client:visible />

A read on a hard disk is three costs in series, and only the last one moves data:

1. **Seek** — the voice-coil actuator swings the arm to the right cylinder: about 0.5–1 ms
   track-to-track, 8–9 ms average on a 7200 rpm 3.5-inch drive, 15–17 ms full stroke. It is a
   settle-limited mechanical motion, so seek time grows roughly with the *square root* of the
   distance, not linearly.
2. **Rotational latency** — you then wait for the sector to come round under the head. At
   7200 rpm a revolution is 8.33 ms, so the average wait is **4.17 ms**; 10 000 rpm buys 3 ms
   and 15 000 rpm buys 2 ms. This is the entire reason 15k SAS drives existed and cost four
   times as much per gigabyte.
3. **Transfer** — data streams under the head at 150–260 MB/s on a modern drive's outer
   tracks. An 8 KB page takes about 40 µs.

Add them up: a random 8 KB read costs roughly `8.5 + 4.2 + 0.04 ≈ 12.7 ms`, which is where
the famous **80–120 IOPS per spindle** comes from. That number has not improved meaningfully
since 2005, because it is bounded by the mass of an actuator arm and the tensile strength of
an aluminium platter, not by lithography.

Now make the next request land on the *adjacent* sector. Seek is zero, rotational latency is
zero — the sector is already arriving under the head — and you pay only transfer. In the
visual above, sequential service time is about 1.4 ms per request against 13.6 ms for uniform
random, and the fraction of the drive's time that actually moved data rises from 5% to 48%.

:::note[Where "sequential is 100× faster" comes from]
The ratio depends entirely on request size, so quote it carefully. Per *request* at database
page sizes it is roughly 10×. In delivered *bandwidth* it is worse than 100×: 100 random 8 KB
reads per second is 800 KB/s against 200 MB/s streaming, a factor of 250. Push the request up
to 1 MB and the 12.7 ms access is amortized over 5 ms of transfer — 57 MB/s, within about 4× of
streaming, and shrinking further with every doubling of the request. That collapse is the design
rule: make far accesses few and large.
:::

### Elevator scheduling

Because the arm is one shared resource, servicing requests in the order the host issued them
is close to the worst possible order. The fix is older than SQL: buffer a window of pending
requests and service them in **track order**, sweeping the arm in one direction. SCAN reverses
at each end; C-SCAN (the "elevator" proper) sweeps one way and snaps back, which keeps latency
fairer for the tracks it just left; LOOK and C-LOOK are the variants that turn around at the
last request rather than the last track.

Drag the elevator window in the visual. On the uniform-random workload, total arm travel falls
from 758 tracks to 221 and average service time from 13.6 ms to 6.8 ms — the drive got twice
as fast because the *host* stopped asking for things in a stupid order. On the sequential
workload the elevator changes nothing, because there is nothing left to reorder.

This is what the Linux block layer still does. `cat /sys/block/sda/queue/scheduler` shows
`mq-deadline` or `bfq` on a rotational device — both sort by sector with a deadline escape
hatch so a starved request eventually jumps the queue. SATA's NCQ lets the drive itself
reorder up to 32 outstanding commands, which it does better than you can because only the
firmware knows the current head position. The relevant hint is
`/sys/block/sdX/queue/rotational`.

:::caution[The scheduler is a liability on NVMe]
Sorting costs CPU and adds latency, and an SSD has no arm to save. The right scheduler for
NVMe is `none`: `echo none > /sys/block/nvme0n1/queue/scheduler`. Distros usually get this
right through udev rules, but virtualised and containerised hosts frequently do not, and
`mq-deadline` on NVMe is a measurable tax on every I/O plus a serialisation point at high
queue depth.
:::

The same geometry explains two engine knobs. InnoDB's `innodb_flush_neighbors=1` flushes dirty
pages that are physically adjacent to the page it was going to flush anyway, because on a
platter their write is nearly free; on an SSD it is pure write amplification and the
recommended setting is `0`. Postgres's `effective_io_concurrency` and InnoDB's
`innodb_io_capacity` are both, underneath, a statement about how many independent far accesses
the device can absorb at once — one for a spindle, hundreds for NVMe.

## NAND: you write pages, you erase blocks

<FlashTranslationLab client:visible />

Flash stores bits as charge trapped on a floating gate, or in a charge-trap layer in 3D NAND.
Three asymmetries follow, and they are the whole story:

- **Read** is fast and fine-grained: one page, 50–90 µs (`tR`).
- **Program** writes a page — 4 KB on older parts, **16 KB** on modern TLC and QLC — and takes
  650–2500 µs (`tPROG`), because multi-level cells are programmed in several passes to place
  the charge precisely.
- **Erase** is the problem. A cell cannot be flipped back from programmed to erased
  individually. You can only erase an entire **block**: hundreds to a couple of thousand pages,
  so **4–24 MB**, in 3–10 ms (`tBERS`).

And a page must be erased before it can be programmed. So the smallest thing the media lets
you overwrite is 4–24 MB, while the smallest thing the host asks you to overwrite is 512 bytes.

Cells also wear out. Each program/erase cycle damages the oxide, and endurance falls hard with
bits per cell: SLC 50 000–100 000 P/E cycles, MLC 3 000–10 000, TLC 1 000–3 000, QLC often a
few hundred to about 1 000. Firmware that erased a block on every 8 KB overwrite would destroy
a QLC drive in days.

## The FTL: a log-structured filesystem in firmware

The **flash translation layer** resolves the mismatch the only way it can. It never overwrites
in place. Every host write goes to the **next free page of the currently open block**, and the
FTL updates a logical-to-physical map so the LBA now points at the new location. The previous
physical page is marked **invalid** — garbage that still occupies space and cannot be reused
until its whole block is erased.

That is why the same overwrite is cheap logically and expensive physically.
`UPDATE t SET c = c + 1 WHERE id = 42` rewrites 8 KB from the host's point of view. Inside the
drive it programmed a fresh 16 KB page, left 16 KB of garbage behind, dirtied a mapping entry,
and moved the device one step closer to the moment it must stop and collect.

A page-level map is not small. At 4 KB granularity with 4-byte entries, a 1 TB drive needs
about **1 GB of map**, which is where the "1 GB of DRAM per TB of capacity" rule of thumb comes
from. DRAM-less consumer drives use coarser hybrid or block-level mapping — cheap, but a small
random write can force a whole block's worth of relocation — or borrow host RAM through NVMe's
**Host Memory Buffer**, typically 32–64 MB. That is one concrete reason a cheap drive falls
apart on a random-write database workload while looking fine in a sequential benchmark.

## Garbage collection, and where write amplification comes from

Eventually the free-page pool runs low and the drive must reclaim. Garbage collection picks a
**victim block**, copies whatever pages in it are still live into the open block, then erases
it. Greedy selection takes the block with the most invalid pages, which is what the simulator
above does; production firmware uses cost-benefit variants that also weight how long a block
has been idle, because a cold block full of garbage is a better victim than a hot one that is
about to become garbage anyway.

Those relocation copies are writes the host never asked for. The ratio

```
write amplification = NAND pages programmed / host pages written
```

determines both how fast the drive is under sustained write load and how long it lives. Watch
the latency trace in the visual: while free pages exist, a write is about 25 µs. The moment a
write arrives and no erased page is waiting for it, that write blocks behind several page
relocations and a block erase, and you see a millisecond spike. This is the origin of SSD
tail latency — p50 stays flat and beautiful while p99.9 walks off into the milliseconds.

Three mitigations, all of which you can move in the visual:

**Over-provisioning.** Physical NAND the host cannot address gives GC room to find blocks that
are mostly garbage. Every drive gets 7.37% for free from the GB-versus-GiB difference: 1024 GiB
of NAND sold as "1024 GB". Enterprise parts ship 960 GB or 800 GB out of that same 1024 GiB,
which the datasheets quote as 7% and 28% of extra over-provisioning on top of that inherent
7.37%. For small over-provisioning the steady-state rule of thumb under random
writes is roughly `WA ≈ 1 / (2 × OP)`. In the simulator the uniform-random workload goes from
**4.1× at 8% over-provisioning to 1.2× at 67%** — same workload, same firmware, four times the
NAND wear. You can buy this on any drive by never partitioning the last 20%: unwritten LBAs are
free space as far as the FTL is concerned.

**TRIM / discard.** When a file is deleted, the filesystem knows those LBAs are dead but the
drive does not — the map still points at live pages, and GC will faithfully relocate data
nobody will ever read again. `TRIM` (SATA) and `DEALLOCATE` (NVMe) tell the drive to drop the
mapping. Turn TRIM off in the visual on the uniform-random workload and write amplification
goes from 4.1× to 6.2× at identical over-provisioning, because the drive's idea of "live" is
now 95 pages instead of 82. In practice: prefer the weekly `fstrim.timer` to the synchronous
`discard` mount option, confirm with `lsblk --discard` that `DISC-GRAN` is non-zero, and know
that discards stop dead at any layer that does not forward them — `dm-crypt` without
`allow_discards`, LVM without `issue_discards=1`, and plenty of thin-provisioned SAN and
hypervisor stacks.

**Wear levelling.** GC must also equalise erase counts, or the blocks holding your hottest
table would burn out while blocks holding a static archive sit at cycle zero. Dynamic wear
levelling only balances among blocks being rewritten; static wear levelling periodically *moves
cold data* out of low-cycle blocks so they can rejoin the rotation — relocations that cost write
amplification for no host benefit at all. The P/E column in the "show the numbers" table under
the visual is what wear levelling exists to keep flat.

:::caution[Fresh-out-of-box benchmarks are worthless]
A new or freshly-TRIMmed drive has every block erased, so GC never runs and write amplification
is 1.0. Numbers from that state describe a device that will not exist ten minutes into
production. Precondition the drive — write the full capacity twice with the workload's own
access pattern — before believing any sustained-write figure. The shape you are looking for is
the one in the latency trace: a flat region, then a cliff when the free pool runs out.
:::

In monitoring, the ground truth is SMART. `nvme smart-log /dev/nvme0` gives `data_units_written`
(host) and `percentage_used` (the controller's own wear estimate against its rated endurance);
`data_units_written` counts 1000 × 512-byte units, so multiply by 512 000 for bytes and compare
it against the rated TBW or DWPD on the datasheet. On SATA, attribute 241
`Total_LBAs_Written` against the vendor's NAND-writes attribute is the same ratio measured end
to end. If host writes are
2 TB and the drive says it wore as if 8 TB were written, your write amplification is 4 and your
five-year drive is a fifteen-month drive.

## Why enterprise drives have capacitors

Every drive has a volatile write cache — DRAM, or an SLC-mode region of the NAND — because
acknowledging a write at DRAM speed is the only way to hit 20 µs. That cache is not durable. A
`FLUSH CACHE` (SATA) or `nvme flush` forces it out, and that is what `fsync()` ultimately turns
into.

An **enterprise drive adds power-loss protection**: a bank of capacitors holding enough energy
for the controller to finish programming everything in flight and write out the FTL map when the
rail drops. Such a drive can honour a flush almost instantly, because its cache is already
effectively non-volatile. A consumer drive without capacitors must actually push the data into
NAND, so a flush costs hundreds of microseconds to milliseconds. This is why the same Postgres
commit benchmark runs an order of magnitude faster on a Samsung PM9A3 or Solidigm D7 than on a
990 Pro with the same nominal IOPS — the benchmark is measuring flush cost, not read or write
cost.

:::danger[The failure mode is silent data loss, not slowness]
Some consumer SSDs and many USB enclosures acknowledge a flush without performing one, and some
FTLs lose the *mapping table* on power cut, taking out data written minutes earlier. Check
`nvme id-ctrl /dev/nvme0 | grep vwc` and `hdparm -W /dev/sda` to see whether a volatile cache is
even declared, and never disable the flush — `hdparm -W0` is a hammer, not a fix, and
XFS removed its `nobarrier` mount option outright in Linux 4.19 for exactly this reason. The
precise promise `fsync` makes, and the places it does not make it, is the next page in this
module.
:::

## ZNS: giving the placement decision back to the engine

The deep absurdity of the FTL is that a log-structured storage engine — RocksDB, or anything
LSM — is already writing large immutable segments in append order, and then a second
log-structured system inside the drive shuffles them again, with less information and its own
garbage collection. Two garbage collectors fighting is where a great deal of real-world write
amplification comes from.

**Zoned Namespaces** (NVMe TP 4053) removes the second one. The drive exposes its media as
**zones** the size of an erase-block group — typically hundreds of MB to a couple of GB — each
with a write pointer. The rules: writes to a zone must be sequential from that pointer, and to
rewrite a zone you must `RESET` it wholesale. That is the raw NAND contract, made visible.

What the engine gets for accepting it: it chooses which data shares a zone, so it can put one
LSM level, or one SSTable, or one table's data in its own zone and reset the whole zone when
that file is dropped. There is then nothing to relocate and device write amplification goes to
approximately 1. It also needs far less over-provisioning and far less map DRAM, since a zone
map is thousands of entries rather than hundreds of millions. RocksDB's **ZenFS** backend does
exactly this: the ZNS paper (Bjørling et al., USENIX ATC 2021) measured roughly 2× the RocksDB
throughput and about 4× lower 99.99th-percentile read latency against a conventional SSD on the
same media. `blkzone report /dev/nvme0n1` prints the zone table; `f2fs` and `btrfs` speak the
protocol, and so do host-managed **SMR** hard drives, whose shingled tracks have the identical
"append-only, reset to rewrite" shape. The two halves of this page converge there.

NVMe **Flexible Data Placement** (TP 4146) is the pragmatic successor: the host tags each write
with a placement handle so data with similar lifetime lands in the same erase blocks, while
ordinary random-write semantics keep working. It gives up most of ZNS's DRAM and
over-provisioning savings and keeps most of the write-amplification win, which is why it is
reaching cloud fleets faster than ZNS did.

## Where it bites in production

| Symptom | What is actually happening | Knob |
|---|---|---|
| Write p99.9 in milliseconds, p50 fine | Host writes blocking behind GC relocation and erase | Raise over-provisioning, lower write rate, check TRIM reaches the device |
| "Fast for 20 minutes, then fell off a cliff" | SLC write cache exhausted; drive folding to TLC/QLC and collecting | Measure after preconditioning; size for steady state |
| Wearing 4× faster than the write volume implies | Write amplification: small random writes, no discard, nearly-full drive | `nvme smart-log`; leave 20% unpartitioned; batch writes in the engine |
| SSD as slow as a disk under `fsync`-heavy load | No power-loss protection, so every flush reaches NAND | A drive with PLP, or at least move the WAL onto one |
| Random reads fine, random writes terrible, cheap drive | DRAM-less FTL with block-level mapping thrashing | A page-mapped drive with real DRAM |
| HDD array stuck at ~100 IOPS regardless of tuning | One arm per spindle: you are buying actuators, not terabytes | More spindles, larger requests, `mq-deadline`, sequential layout |

And the design consequence the rest of this site keeps returning to: an engine that converts
random writes into large sequential appends is not merely being polite to a disk arm. On flash
it is aligning its write pattern with the erase-block granularity, which lowers device write
amplification, shrinks the FTL's work, and flattens the latency tail. That is the strongest
single argument for a write-ahead log, and for LSM trees over update-in-place B-trees on
write-heavy workloads.

:::note[What to take forward]
A hard disk charges you for arm movement and rotation, so sequential access is worth
restructuring an engine around. Flash charges you for the mismatch between the 16 KB page it
programs and the 4–24 MB block it erases: the FTL turns every overwrite into an append plus
garbage, and garbage collection pays for it later in write amplification and millisecond
latency spikes. Over-provisioning, TRIM and large sequential writes are the three levers you
actually control — and ZNS is the hardware conceding that the storage engine knew better all
along.
:::
