Between your engine and the NAND sits a cache you did not write, an eviction policy you cannot
influence and a writeback timer you cannot see. Every storage engine has to decide whether to
cooperate with that layer, work around it, or bypass it entirely — and the decision shows up in
the syscalls it issues, in how it recovers from a crash, and in whether a page fault can stall a
thread in the middle of a critical section.

## The copy path

A buffered `read()` on Linux resolves to `filemap_read()`, which looks the file offset up in an
XArray hanging off `inode->i_mapping->i_pages` — that lookup table *is* the page cache. A hit
copies the folio into your buffer with `copy_folio_to_iter()` and returns; nothing touches the
device. A miss calls into the readahead machinery, sleeps until the I/O completes, then copies.

`write()` is `generic_perform_write()`: find or allocate the folio for that offset, copy your
bytes in, mark the folio dirty, tag it dirty in the XArray, call
`balance_dirty_pages_ratelimited()`, return. The return value tells you how many bytes were
copied **into volatile RAM**. Nothing has been submitted to the device. Nothing is durable. That
is the single most important sentence about `write()`.

Three consequences worth having in your hands:

- **Every buffered access costs a memcpy.** ~300–500 ns per 4 KiB page, each way. An engine that
  also caches the page holds it twice — once in the page cache, once in its own buffer pool. This
  is *double buffering*, and it is why PostgreSQL's `shared_buffers` rule of thumb is 25% of RAM
  rather than 80%: the kernel is expected to hold the other copy.
- **A partial-page write can cost a read.** If you write 100 bytes into a page that is not
  resident, the kernel has to read the surrounding 4 KiB from disk first to have something to
  modify — read-modify-write. Engines write whole aligned pages for exactly this reason.
- **`read()`/`write()` mutate the shared file offset.** `f_pos` lives in `struct file`, shared by
  every thread and every process holding that descriptor, so `lseek()` + `read()` from two threads
  is a race. `pread()`/`pwrite()` take the offset as an argument and touch no shared state — which
  is why PostgreSQL backends, InnoDB and essentially every threaded engine use the positional
  forms exclusively, and the vectored `preadv()`/`pwritev()` when they want to move several
  adjacent blocks in one call.

:::caution[write() returning success is not a durability signal]
It means "copied into the page cache and marked dirty". The bytes survive a process crash — the
page cache belongs to the kernel, not to you — but a power cut loses them. Only `fsync()`,
`fdatasync()` or opening with `O_SYNC` changes that, and [what fsync actually
promises](/p01-foundations/hardware-os-durability/06-fsync-write-barriers-and-the-durability-contract/)
is narrower than most people assume.
:::

## Dirty pages, writeback timers and the throttle

A dirty folio sits on its inode's dirty list until one of four things happens.

1. **The periodic flusher.** A per-backing-device kworker wakes every
   `dirty_writeback_centisecs` (default 500 = 5 s) and writes back anything dirtied longer ago
   than `dirty_expire_centisecs` (default 3000 = 30 s). So a write that returned half a minute ago
   can still be RAM-only.
2. **The background threshold.** Once dirty memory exceeds `vm.dirty_background_ratio` (default
   10% of dirtyable memory) the flusher starts immediately. Writers do not wait.
3. **The hard threshold.** Once dirty memory exceeds `vm.dirty_ratio` (default 20%),
   `balance_dirty_pages()` throttles the *writing process*: it sleeps proportionally to how far
   over the limit the system is, and may be made to do writeback itself. The symptom is a
   process stuck in `D` state with `balance_dirty_pages` in `/proc/PID/stack`, and a latency
   spike across every writer on the box, not just the greedy one.
4. **Reclaim.** Memory pressure evicts page-cache pages; a dirty victim must be written out
   first, which turns an allocation into a synchronous write.

Push the `dirty_ratio` slider down in the visual above and write a few pages: the throttle fires
and the caller starts paying for writeback it did not ask for. That is the production failure in
miniature.

Two tuning notes that follow directly. On a large machine the *ratios* are the wrong unit — 10%
of 512 GB is 51 GB of dirty data that some unlucky `fsync()` will eventually have to wait for, so
production database hosts set the absolute forms (`vm.dirty_background_bytes` ≈ 64–256 MB,
`vm.dirty_bytes` ≈ 512 MB–2 GB) to keep writeback continuous instead of cliff-shaped. And
PostgreSQL's `checkpoint_completion_target = 0.9`, `checkpoint_flush_after = 256kB` and
`bgwriter_flush_after` exist purely to spread this out: the flush-after knobs call
`sync_file_range()` to push pages toward the device early so the checkpoint's final `fsync()`
does not meet a mountain of dirty pages at once.

:::danger[The kernel does not know your WAL rule]
Writeback ordering is the kernel's business, not yours. The flusher will happily write a dirty
data page to disk before the log record describing that change is durable — it has never heard of
write-ahead logging. With `write()` you at least control *when the page enters the cache*, so the
engine can hold a dirty page back until the log is flushed. With `mmap` you lose even that, which
is the first and worst of the objections below.
:::

## Readahead: a small state machine that random access defeats

Per open file, the kernel keeps a `struct file_ra_state`: `start`, `size`, `async_size`,
`ra_pages`, `mmap_miss`, `prev_pos`. `ra_pages` comes from the block device's
`read_ahead_kb` — 128 KB by default, so 32 pages of 4 KiB.

`ondemand_readahead()` classifies each miss:

- **Start of a sequence** (this page follows the previous access): seed the window with
  `get_init_ra_size()` — a handful of pages — and issue one request for the whole window.
- **Continuation** (the page falls inside `[start, start + size)`): **double** the window, capped
  at `ra_pages`. Sequential reading ramps 4 → 8 → 16 → 32 pages and then streams.
- **Neither**: treat it as a standalone random read. One request, exactly the pages asked for, and
  the ramp resets. This is the correct behaviour and it is why buffered random reads are not
  silently amplified 16×.

The clever part is the asynchronous handoff. The kernel sets the `PG_readahead` flag on one page
inside the window (at `start + size - async_size`). When a reader *hits* that page in cache, the
kernel fires the next window in the background and returns immediately — so a sequential scan
never stalls after the first miss. Click sequentially through the visual and watch the window
double and the marker move ahead of you; then click randomly and watch it collapse to one page
per access.

You can override the heuristic:

| Hint | Effect |
|---|---|
| `posix_fadvise(POSIX_FADV_SEQUENTIAL)` | Doubles `ra_pages` for this descriptor |
| `posix_fadvise(POSIX_FADV_RANDOM)` | Sets readahead to zero — demand paging only |
| `posix_fadvise(POSIX_FADV_WILLNEED)` | Starts the read now; the classic poor-man's async I/O |
| `posix_fadvise(POSIX_FADV_DONTNEED)` | Drops clean pages — what backup tools use so a full-file read does not evict your working set |
| `madvise(MADV_RANDOM / MADV_WILLNEED)` | The same hints for a mapping |

PostgreSQL used `POSIX_FADV_WILLNEED` for years to prefetch bitmap-heap-scan and vacuum blocks,
with `effective_io_concurrency` controlling how many hints were in flight. PostgreSQL 17 replaced
the ad-hoc calls with the streaming read API and `io_combine_limit` (merge adjacent block reads,
default 128 kB), and PostgreSQL 18 added a real AIO subsystem: `io_method` = `sync`, `worker`
(default, with `io_workers` = 3) or `io_uring`, and a default `effective_io_concurrency` raised
from 1 to 16. InnoDB, which runs with `O_DIRECT` and therefore gets no kernel readahead at all,
implements its own: linear read-ahead fires when `innodb_read_ahead_threshold` (default 56) of the
64 pages in an extent have been touched in order, plus an off-by-default
`innodb_random_read_ahead`.

## mmap: a load instruction that can go to the device

`mmap(NULL, len, PROT_READ | PROT_WRITE, MAP_SHARED, fd, 0)` creates a VMA and installs no page
tables at all. The first touch of each page traps into `filemap_fault()`:

- **Minor fault** — the folio is already in the page cache, so the kernel only installs a PTE
  (~1 µs). On the way out, `fault_around` maps up to `fault_around_bytes` (64 KiB, 16 pages) of
  already-cached neighbours for free, which is why the pages after a fault feel instant.
- **Major fault** — the folio is not resident, and the faulting thread blocks *inside the load
  instruction* until the device answers.

One asymmetry catches people: a cold mmap fault does **not** do the careful ramp that `read()`
does. `do_sync_mmap_readahead()` reads the entire `ra_pages` window on the first miss unless the
VMA is marked `VM_RAND_READ` via `madvise(MADV_RANDOM)`, and it only stops once `mmap_miss` in that
descriptor's `file_ra_state` — incremented on a wasted fault, decremented on a useful one —
climbs past `MMAP_LOTSAMISS`, which is 100. A random-access mmap workload therefore pulls
128 KB per 4 KiB you wanted, until the heuristic notices. Toggle `MADV_RANDOM` in the visual
and compare the "device reads" and "readahead wasted" counters.

The timeline above is the argument that matters. A buffer pool knows whether a page is resident,
because residency is a field in its own hash table: it can discover the miss, issue the read, and
only then take the page latch. The MMU has no such courtesy — it discovers the miss *while
executing your load*, which is to say inside whatever critical section you were in. Raise the miss
rate or pick a slower device and watch the latch convoy form: throughput stops scaling with worker
count and starts tracking one thread's I/O latency.

## The four arguments against mmap

Crotty, Leis and Pavlo made the case directly in *Are You Sure You Want to Use MMAP in Your
Database Management System?* (CIDR 2022). Their four problems, in the order they bite:

1. **Transactional safety.** You cannot stop the kernel from writing a dirty mapped page to disk.
   `msync()` flushes a range on demand, but there is no way to say "not yet" — so WAL-before-data
   cannot be enforced by holding the page back. Nor is there ordering *between* ranges, nor
   any atomicity: an 8 KiB page spanning two 4 KiB folios can reach the device half-written.
2. **I/O stalls.** Any load can fault. `mincore()` can report residency but the answer is stale
   the moment it returns, and there is no async fault — so every access is a potential
   unbounded stall at a point you did not choose. The visual above is this argument.
3. **Error handling.** A failed read behind a load arrives as `SIGBUS`, not an error return.
   Touching a mapped region after the file is truncated: also `SIGBUS`. Handling it means
   `longjmp`-ing out of a signal handler with latches held. And because there is no "read" event,
   there is no natural place to verify a page checksum — you would have to validate on every
   single access.
4. **Performance.** Page-table work does not scale. Evicting a mapped page means clearing PTEs in
   every process that maps it and sending a TLB-shootdown IPI to every core that might have cached
   the translation, with the initiator spinning until all of them acknowledge. Their measurements
   show mmap throughput collapsing once the working set exceeds RAM and refusing to scale past a
   few threads, while `pread()` with `O_DIRECT` scales linearly and saturates the drives. Press
   `munmap()` in the first visual to see the shootdown cost accounted; the kernel pays the same
   price on every eviction of a mapped page.

To that list add the one you feel first in production: **no eviction control**. The kernel's LRU
does not know that this page is a B-tree root and that one is the last block of a bulk load. It
will evict the root. `mlock()` is the only override, it needs `RLIMIT_MEMLOCK` headroom, and it
pins whole ranges rather than expressing a policy.

## Who maps files anyway, and why they get away with it

| System | How it uses mmap | Why it is safe there |
|---|---|---|
| LMDB | The whole database is mapped; read-only by default (`MDB_WRITEMAP` optional) | Copy-on-write B+tree, single writer, pages never overwritten in place; a commit writes new pages, `fsync`s, then flips one of two meta pages and `fsync`s again. There is no ordering left for the kernel to get wrong |
| SQLite | `PRAGMA mmap_size = N`; reads served from the map, writes still via `write()` | Rollback journal / WAL still provides atomicity. The docs state the risks plainly: a stray application pointer can corrupt the file, and I/O errors arrive as `SIGBUS` |
| Lucene / Elasticsearch | `MMapDirectory` over immutable segment files | Segments are written with ordinary I/O and never modified afterwards. Mapping is a read path only |
| MongoDB (MMAPv1) | Mapped data files with a journal | Deprecated in 4.0, removed in 4.2, replaced by WiredTiger and its own cache — the clearest verdict in the table |
| MonetDB, RavenDB (Voron) | Mapped columnar/data files | Read-mostly, in-memory-sized working sets |

The invariant they share: **the mapped bytes are immutable, or durability is provided by a
separate ordered mechanism.** mmap is a fine way to read an append-only file. It is a bad way to
implement an update-in-place page store.

## The other philosophy: the engine owns its cache

| Engine | Cache ownership | I/O path | Knobs |
|---|---|---|---|
| InnoDB | Buffer pool with midpoint-insertion LRU (`innodb_old_blocks_pct` = 37, `innodb_old_blocks_time` = 1000 ms) so a scan cannot flush hot pages | `O_DIRECT`, native AIO, own linear read-ahead, doublewrite for torn pages | `innodb_flush_method=O_DIRECT`, `innodb_buffer_pool_size`, `innodb_read_ahead_threshold` |
| PostgreSQL | `shared_buffers` plus deliberate reliance on the kernel page cache (double buffering accepted) | Buffered `pread`/`pwrite`, `sync_file_range` hints, AIO since 18 | `shared_buffers`, `effective_io_concurrency`, `io_method`, `io_combine_limit`, `debug_io_direct` |
| RocksDB | Block cache over immutable SSTables | Optional `use_direct_reads`, `use_direct_io_for_flush_and_compaction` | `block_cache_size`, `compaction_readahead_size` |
| LMDB | None — the page cache *is* the cache | `mmap` reads, `write()` writes, two `fsync`s per commit | `mapsize`, `MDB_NOSYNC` family |

The direction of travel is unmistakable. PostgreSQL, the last major holdout for kernel-managed
caching, added `debug_io_direct` in 16, the streaming read API in 17 and the AIO subsystem in 18;
InnoDB has run `O_DIRECT` for a decade. The reason is not that the page cache is badly written —
it is that an engine knows things the kernel cannot: which page is a B-tree root, which scan is a
one-shot, which write must not precede which log flush. [Direct I/O and
io_uring](/p01-foundations/hardware-os-durability/09-direct-i-o-vs-buffered-i-o-and-io-uring/)
takes this apart properly.

## Where it bites in production

- **A wall of dirty pages meets `fsync()`.** `grep -e Dirty -e Writeback /proc/meminfo` on a busy
  host; if `Dirty` is tens of gigabytes, some checkpoint is going to wait for all of it. Fix with
  `vm.dirty_background_bytes` / `vm.dirty_bytes` and the engine's flush-after knobs.
- **Everything goes slow at once, and `iostat` looks fine.** Writers throttled in
  `balance_dirty_pages()`; look for `D` state and `/proc/PID/stack`. The process that crossed the
  threshold is not necessarily the one you notice.
- **Read amplification on a mapped store.** 128 KB pulled per 4 KiB touched, until `mmap_miss`
  gives up. `MADV_RANDOM`, or lower `read_ahead_kb` for that device.
- **`SIGBUS` in the logs.** Either a media error or a file truncated under a live mapping. There
  is no partial-failure story: the process dies.
- **Double buffering wasting RAM.** With a 25%-of-RAM buffer pool plus the page cache holding the
  same blocks, your effective cache is smaller than your RAM. `vmtouch` or `fincore` will show you
  exactly what the kernel is holding.
- **A backup evicts the working set.** A full-file read through the page cache pushes out
  everything hot. `posix_fadvise(POSIX_FADV_DONTNEED)` after each chunk is the standard fix, and
  the reason good backup tools use it.

:::note[What to take forward]
`write()` means "copied into the page cache and marked dirty" — writeback happens on the kernel's
schedule, throttles the writer at `vm.dirty_ratio`, and respects no ordering rule of yours. The
readahead state machine rewards sequential access and correctly refuses to help random access,
while a cold mmap fault does the opposite until you tell it `MADV_RANDOM`. And mmap's central
defect is not speed but control: a load can fault to the device inside a critical section, the
kernel picks the eviction victim, an I/O error is a signal, and there is no way to order
writeback — which is why engines that must respect write-ahead logging own their cache instead.
:::
