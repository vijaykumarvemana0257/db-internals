`fsync` tells you the bytes reached durable media. It says nothing about *which* bytes, or
how many. A storage device makes exactly one atomicity promise — a single sector is written
whole or not at all — and every engine writes pages four to thirty-two times that size. Pull
the power in the middle and the platter holds a page that is half the old image and half the
new one: a state the database was never in, and one the write-ahead log cannot repair by
itself.

## The sector is the only atomic unit

A drive's sector — 512 bytes on legacy media, 4096 on modern — is the unit its firmware
writes as one indivisible operation. On a spinning disk the drive holds enough rotational
energy, and on an enterprise SSD enough capacitance, to finish the sector in flight when
power disappears. Nothing above that line is promised by anyone:

- **The OS splits your `pwrite`.** A 16 KB buffered write becomes several 4 KB page-cache
  pages, flushed by different `bio`s, possibly to different places in the I/O queue.
- **The device splits it again.** A 16 KB command is a burst of sector programs; the NAND
  FTL may place them in different erase blocks on different dies.
- **RAID splits it a third time.** A page that straddles a RAID-5 stripe boundary is two
  member writes plus a parity update — the classic *write hole*, where a crash leaves data
  and parity disagreeing even if every sector was individually fine.
- **512e media lies about the unit.** A "512-byte sector" drive built on 4 K media emulates
  512 B logically and does read-modify-write underneath, so the true atomic unit is 4 KB
  while the kernel reports 512. Check both:
  `cat /sys/block/nvme0n1/queue/{logical,physical}_block_size`, or `lsblk -o NAME,LOG-SEC,PHY-SEC`.

| Engine page | On 4Kn media | On 512e media | Can it tear? |
|---|---|---|---|
| SQLite 4 KB | 1 sector | 8 sectors | Not on 4Kn |
| PostgreSQL 8 KB (`BLCKSZ`) | 2 sectors | 16 sectors | Yes |
| InnoDB 16 KB (`innodb_page_size`) | 4 sectors | 32 sectors | Yes |
| InnoDB 64 KB | 16 sectors | 128 sectors | Yes, badly |

Drag the crash slider in the simulator and the page-size chooser together: the tear only
disappears when the page collapses to a single sector. That equivalence is why SQLite's
4 KB default page on 4Kn storage is unusually safe, and why "use `innodb_page_size=4k`" is
a real (if bandwidth-hostile) torn-write mitigation.

:::caution[NVMe does not make this go away]
Flash has no moving parts, which tempts people into assuming atomicity. It has an FTL
instead: a power cut mid-program can corrupt the NAND page being written, and on some
controllers the *containing erase block*. Consumer drives usually have no power-loss
protection capacitors at all — their write cache is volatile, and the only reason they look
safe is that `fsync` forces a cache flush. Modern NVMe does expose a real guarantee, in the
Identify Controller fields `AWUPF` (atomic write unit, power fail) and `NAWUPF`; Linux
surfaces it as `RWF_ATOMIC` on `pwritev2` with XFS forced alignment. That is a promise you
can query, not one you can assume.
:::

## Why the log cannot repair the page

Redo records are not pictures of pages; they are **deltas against a specific prior state**.
ARIES calls this physiological logging, and PostgreSQL, InnoDB and every descendant use it
because it is compact: "on block 4711, insert this 62-byte tuple at line pointer 14, move
`pd_upper` down by 64" is far smaller than 8 KB. Replay is made idempotent by stamping every
page with the LSN of the last record applied to it — redo applies a record only when the
record's LSN exceeds the page's `pageLSN`.

Both halves of that scheme break on a torn page, and the simulator lets you produce each
failure deliberately, because **the `pageLSN` lives in the header, which lives in sector 0**:

- **Sector 0 landed.** The page now advertises the *new* LSN while later sectors still hold
  old bytes. Redo compares LSNs, concludes the page is already current, and skips every
  record that would have fixed it. Recovery completes, reports success, and the corruption is
  permanent and silent.
- **Sector 0 did not land.** The page advertises the old LSN, so redo does replay — onto a
  base image that is already partly new. "Insert at the offset in `pd_upper`" now writes into
  bytes that the half-applied update already moved. The result is not the old page, not the
  new page, and not anything the log can describe.

The invariant redo depends on is that the on-disk page is *some consistent past state of that
page*. A torn page is not a past state; it is a state that never existed. No amount of log is
a remedy, which is why the defenses in the last section all work by making sure recovery
starts from an intact base image rather than by making redo smarter.

:::note[The LSN is a torn-page detector too — sometimes]
Old InnoDB formats wrote the log sequence number twice: `FIL_PAGE_LSN` at page offset 16 and
its low four bytes again in the eight-byte page trailer, `FIL_PAGE_END_LSN_OLD_CHKSUM`. Head
and tail land in different sectors, so a mismatch is proof of a tear. This catches a tear
that straddles the two, and misses one entirely contained between them — which is precisely
why it was a supplement to the checksum, never a replacement.
:::

## Checksums detect; they never repair

A per-page checksum is computed over the page bytes just before the write and stored inside
the page. On every read the engine recomputes and compares. The two shipping designs:

| | PostgreSQL | InnoDB |
|---|---|---|
| Field | `pd_checksum`, 2 bytes in `PageHeaderData` | `FIL_PAGE_SPACE_OR_CHKSUM`, 4 bytes at offset 0 (+ trailer in old formats) |
| Algorithm | FNV-1a-derived: 32 parallel lanes over the 8 KB block, xor-folded, xored with the block number, then `% 65535 + 1` | CRC-32C (Castagnoli), `innodb_checksum_algorithm=crc32`; `full_crc32` since 8.0.17 covers the whole page |
| Width | **16 bits** | **32 bits** |
| Turning it on | `initdb -k`, or `pg_checksums --enable` with the cluster down; default *on* from PG 18 | On by default since 5.7 |
| On failure | `WARNING: page verification failed, calculated checksum 34177 but expected 8952` then `ERROR: invalid page in block 4711 of relation base/16384/16427` | `InnoDB: Database page corruption on disk or a failed file read of page`, then the server aborts |
| Monitoring | `pg_stat_database.checksum_failures`, `checksum_last_failure` | error log, `innochecksum` offline |

The checksum field is excluded from its own computation — PostgreSQL zeroes `pd_checksum`
before hashing and writes the result back — which is why the verifier in the simulator
recomputes over a page with word 0 blanked. Two consequences follow immediately. First, the
stored value comes from whichever sector 0 landed, so a tear puts an old checksum next to new
bytes or a new checksum next to old bytes; either way it mismatches. Second, and this is the
whole point of the mechanism: **a mismatch aborts the read.** It does not tell you which
sectors are wrong, and it cannot reconstruct them. The checksum is a smoke alarm.

<ChecksumWidthEscapeLab client:visible />

Width is what sets the miss rate. PostgreSQL's 16 bits let roughly 1 corrupt page in 65,535
through; a 1 TB cluster is about 134 million 8 KB pages, so at that scale the field is a
statistical filter, not a guarantee. CRC-32C's 1 in 4.29 billion is why InnoDB, ZFS and every
format designed after 2005 spend the extra two bytes — and why CRC rather than a truncated
hash: a degree-32 CRC provably detects *every* single-bit flip and every burst error up to 32
bits, while a folded FNV sum only gets the average case. On modern CPUs this costs nothing
extra; the `crc32` instruction from SSE 4.2 does a quadword per cycle.

## Bit rot and misdirected writes: corruption with no crash

Torn pages need a crash. The other two failure modes do not, and they are the reason a
checksum earns its keep during normal operation:

- **Bit rot / latent sector errors.** Charge leaks out of NAND cells, a platter sector degrades,
  a cable or HBA flips a bit in flight. NetApp's study of 1.53 million drives (Bairavasundaram
  et al., FAST '08) found checksum mismatches on 0.86% of nearline SATA drives and 0.06% of
  enterprise drives over 17 months — rare per drive, routine per fleet.
- **Misdirected writes.** The firmware writes the right bytes to the wrong LBA. The landing
  page is internally perfect; it is just the wrong page. Set the simulator to *misdirected
  write* and untick the block-number box to watch every single one sail through a
  content-only checksum.

The answer is to make the page **self-describing**. PostgreSQL xors the block number into the
sum before folding, so a page that arrives at the wrong block number cannot verify — but
`pd_checksum` contains no relfilenode, so relation A block 5 landing on relation B block 5 is
still invisible. InnoDB stores `FIL_PAGE_OFFSET` and `FIL_PAGE_SPACE_ID` inside the page and
checks both on read. ZFS goes furthest: the checksum of a block lives in its *parent* block
pointer, never in the block itself, so a write that destroys the data cannot also rewrite the
checksum that guards it — the end-to-end argument (Saltzer, Reed and Clark, 1984) applied to
storage. That placement is what lets ZFS not merely detect but *repair*, by fetching a good
copy from a mirror, RAID-Z parity, or a ditto block.

Detection also has to be scheduled, because a page nobody reads is never verified:

| Scrub | Scope | Runs online? |
|---|---|---|
| `zpool scrub` / `btrfs scrub` | Every block, repairs from redundancy | Yes |
| `echo check > /sys/block/md0/md/sync_action` | MD RAID consistency | Yes |
| `pg_checksums --check` | Every page of a PostgreSQL cluster | No — cluster must be down |
| `pg_amcheck`, `amcheck` | Heap and B-tree *structure*, not just checksums | Yes |
| `innochecksum` | One `.ibd` file | No — tablespace must be quiescent |
| Backup verification (`pg_basebackup`) | Every page it copies, reports failures | Yes |

:::danger[The checksum can certify garbage]
A checksum is computed in RAM, over a buffer in RAM. If non-ECC memory flips a bit in the
buffer *before* the checksum is computed, the engine faithfully stamps a valid checksum onto
corrupt data and writes it down. Every layer below will agree the page is fine, forever. This
is not a hypothetical — it is the standard argument for ECC memory on database servers, and
it is why T10 DIF/DIX exists: 8 bytes of protection information (guard, application tag,
reference tag) attached per sector and verified by the HBA and the drive, so the check travels
with the data instead of being recomputed at each hop.
:::

## The defenses, in one table

The internals of these belong to the write-ahead logging and recovery module; what matters
here is that each one exists solely to give recovery an **intact base image**.

| Defense | Engine | How it works | What it costs |
|---|---|---|---|
| `full_page_writes` | PostgreSQL (default `on`) | The first modification of a page after each checkpoint logs the entire 8 KB image into the WAL; replay overwrites the whole page, so the torn version on disk is irrelevant | WAL volume — frequently the dominant term. Mitigate with `wal_compression=lz4`/`zstd` and longer `checkpoint_timeout`, not by turning it off |
| Doublewrite buffer | InnoDB (default `on`) | Every page is written first to a sequential doublewrite area (separate `*.dblwr` files since 8.0.20, tuned by `innodb_doublewrite_pages`/`_batch_size`), fsynced, then to its home; recovery restores any page failing its checksum from the copy | Double the data writes, though the extra one is sequential. `innodb_doublewrite=DETECT_ONLY` (8.0.30+) keeps detection and drops recovery |
| Copy-on-write | ZFS, Btrfs, LMDB, LSM engines | Never overwrite a live block. The torn write lands where nothing points yet; the commit is a single-sector root/uberblock switch, which *is* atomic | Space amplification, fragmentation, and a rewritten storage layer |
| Journal the original | SQLite rollback journal / WAL mode | Copy the pre-image out and fsync it before overwriting; `PSOW` (powersafe overwrite) lets it skip journaling when the filesystem promises only the touched sectors are damaged | Extra write per page per transaction |
| Hardware atomicity | NVMe `AWUPF`, `RWF_ATOMIC` on XFS | Ask the device for a genuinely atomic 16 KB write | Requires a drive, filesystem and kernel that all agree — and then you can safely disable the layers above |

:::caution[The two knobs people turn off and regret]
`full_page_writes=off` and `innodb_doublewrite=0` both make write-heavy benchmarks visibly
faster, and both are safe *only* if your storage stack provides page-granularity atomicity —
ZFS with `recordsize` at or above the page size, or hardware atomic writes you have actually
verified. Nothing warns you when the assumption is wrong: the cluster runs perfectly for
months and then fails to recover from one unplanned power loss. If you cannot write down the
sentence "our 8 KB writes are atomic because ___", leave them on.
:::

## Where it bites in production

The symptom is a read that suddenly fails on a page the database has served for months:
`ERROR: invalid page in block 1234 of relation base/16384/16427`, or an InnoDB start-up abort
on page corruption, or `pg_stat_database.checksum_failures` ticking up while everything else
looks healthy. The reflex to resist is "the disk is dying". The checksum only says *these
bytes are not what was written* — the culprit is as often RAM, an HBA, a filesystem bug, or a
storage driver as the media. Before touching anything: check EDAC/`mcelog` for ECC events,
`smartctl -a`, and `dmesg` for I/O errors, and take a physical copy of the file.

Then the recovery path depends entirely on what the page belongs to. Map it with
`SELECT relname, relkind FROM pg_class WHERE relfilenode = 16427`. If it is an index, you are
lucky — `REINDEX` rebuilds it from the heap and the data was never at risk. If it is a heap
page, the honest options are restore-and-PITR, or accept data loss: `zero_damaged_pages=on`
replaces the unreadable page with an empty one and lets the scan continue, silently dropping
every row it held. `ignore_checksum_failure=on` is worse than it sounds — it downgrades the
error to a warning and hands the corrupt page to the executor, where it can propagate into
indexes, materialized views and your next backup.

:::note[What to take forward]
Only a sector is atomic, so any page larger than a sector can land half-written, and that
half-written page is a state the database was never in — which is exactly why redo, whose
records are deltas against a real prior state, cannot repair it. Per-page checksums
(PostgreSQL's 16-bit `pd_checksum`, InnoDB's CRC-32C) turn silent corruption into a loud
error, and scrubbing plus self-describing pages extend that to bit rot and misdirected writes,
but detection is all you get. Repair requires an intact base image, which is what
`full_page_writes`, the doublewrite buffer and copy-on-write each buy in their own currency.
:::
