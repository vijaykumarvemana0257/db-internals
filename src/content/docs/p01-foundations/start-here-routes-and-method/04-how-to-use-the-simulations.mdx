Every page on this site has at least one simulator, and it is easy to use them wrong: drag a
slider, watch something move, feel like you understood it, and move on. That produces the
sensation of learning without much of the substance. The simulators are built for a different
loop, and it takes about a minute to practise.

## The loop: predict, run, compare

1. **Change one input.** Not three. If you move the pool size, the policy and the workload at
   once, you cannot tell which one caused what you see.
2. **Predict the internal state before you run it.** Say it concretely: *this page gets evicted*,
   *the hit rate goes down*, *this transaction blocks*. A vague prediction ("something changes")
   cannot be wrong, so it teaches you nothing.
3. **Run it and compare against the state panel.** When your prediction was right, you have
   evidence your model of the mechanism is right. When it was wrong, you have found the exact
   point where your model and the mechanism disagree — which is the most valuable thing a page can
   give you.

The tour above is that loop on the smallest possible buffer pool: three frames and a fixed
sequence of ten page accesses. Before each access, pick what you think will happen — a hit, or a
miss that evicts a particular page — then reveal it. The frames are drawn in eviction order, with
the next victim on the right, so you have everything you need to predict correctly.

Go through it once under **LRU**. Then switch to **FIFO** and go through the same sequence again.
The first four accesses produce the same outcomes — three misses into free frames, then a hit — and at the fifth access — page D arriving to a full
pool — the two policies evict *different* pages: LRU throws out B, the page that has gone longest
without being used, while FIFO throws out A, the page loaded earliest, even though A was just
accessed. Everything else is identical: same pages, same order, same pool. That single rule change
is also why FIFO ends the sequence with fewer hits than LRU. If you predicted the fifth access
correctly both times, you understand the difference between the two policies better than a
paragraph describing them would give you.

The [buffer pool module](../../../p02-storage-engines/buffer-pool/03-replacement-policies-lru-clock-lru-k-2q-and-arc/)
does this properly, with CLOCK, LRU-K, 2Q and ARC on realistic workloads. The point here is only the
method.

## What the simulators model — and what they do not

The simulators model **mechanism**: which page is evicted, which version a transaction sees, which
log record must reach disk before which page, which replica is allowed to answer. When a simulator
says a design takes two disk reads where another takes one, that relationship is the point, and you
should trust it.

They do **not** model the absolute performance of your hardware or your production system. A
latency or throughput number in a simulator comes from a simple, stated model — usually a handful
of constants for access cost and transfer cost — so that *relative* costs and *shapes* are right: a
cliff where the working set outgrows memory, a gap that closes as request size grows, a stall when a
threshold is crossed. Do not read a simulator's "3,000 IOPS" as a prediction for your machine. Read
"roughly ten times slower, and it falls off a cliff here" as the lesson.

Two more things they deliberately leave out:

- **Most of the concurrency.** Real engines interleave thousands of operations. Simulators usually
  step through a small number, one at a time, so you can see each step. When a page is about a race,
  it will say so and let you control the interleaving.
- **Randomness.** Every simulator is deterministic: the same inputs always produce the same result,
  including anything that looks random. That is what makes predicting possible, and it means a
  surprising result is reproducible — reset and run it again.

## Reading the visual conventions

Every simulator has a **legend**, and the legend is authoritative for that simulator. Beyond that,
a few conventions hold across the whole site, so that the same idea looks the same everywhere:

- **Committed, durable data** and **dirty, uncommitted or in-memory-only data** each have a
  reserved color, and pages use it for that meaning. Learn what makes a page dirty on the
  durability pages, and the same color marks dirty state on the buffer-pool, crash-safety and
  transaction pages that follow. The tour
  above deliberately does *not* use those two colors: a page just read from disk is neither.
- **Dead or garbage data** — a dead tuple version, a tombstone past its grace period, an evicted
  page's former contents — is drawn muted.
- **Status colors** for good, warning, serious and critical are used only for a system's state,
  and always with a label or icon beside them, never on their own.
- **Identity is never carried by color alone.** Anything distinguished by color is also labelled,
  shaped or listed, and most simulators have a **Show the numbers** table underneath with the same
  information as text.

If a simulator ever seems to contradict its page — the page says "watch X happen" and X does not
happen at those settings — that is a bug, not a subtlety, and it is worth reporting.

:::note[What to take forward]
Change one input, predict the internal state out loud, then compare. Trust simulators for mechanism
and relative cost, never for absolute numbers, and treat each legend as the key for its own figure.
:::
