This site is written in one order — foundations, storage engines, query processing,
transactions, then distributed systems — and that order is a good default. It is not the only
correct one. An application engineer who needs to fix a slow query this week should not be
told to read about flash translation layers and latch-free reclamation first. A distributed-systems
engineer needs a specific slice of single-node material, not all of it.

What makes an alternative route *safe* is the prerequisite graph. Every module declares which
modules it assumes, and a route is only honest if it includes everything its modules assume. So
instead of describing routes in prose, the explorer below computes them.

## How to read the map

Each chip is a module, grouped by part. The colored bar on its left says why it is lit:

- **On the route** — a module the route names explicitly.
- **Prerequisite the route pulls in** — a module nobody listed, but that something on the route
  declares it needs. These are the modules a hand-written reading list usually forgets.
- **Dashed border** — the module is specified but not written yet. The site is being built
  progressively, so the totals show both how much a route needs and how much of it you can read
  today. Dashed chips are never links.

Hover any chip to ring its direct prerequisites and see how many later modules build on it. That
second number is a good proxy for how foundational a module is: the ones with long tails are the
ones not to skim.

## The three engineering routes

**Application engineer.** For someone who writes queries and schemas for a living and needs to
make them fast and correct. It runs through the SQL foundations, then straight into how a plan is
built and read — logical plans, physical operators, cost-based optimization, EXPLAIN — then index
selection, then the transaction and concurrency material that decides whether your code has race
conditions, and finally the using-it-well modules on access patterns, pooling and schema change.
It does not skip storage internals as cleanly as you might hope — select it and look at Part 2.
Physical operators and index selection assume you know how pages, the buffer pool, B-trees, LSM
trees and the other index structures behave, so those light up as prerequisites. What the route
genuinely skips is the *deeper* storage material: latch coupling and latch-free trees, compaction
internals, tries and vector indexes, column stores. You need a B-tree's cost shape, not how its
latches are coupled. A reasonable place to stop is after the using-it-well leg; the operating leg
is there for when you start owning the database, not just writing to it.

**Storage internals.** For someone who wants to understand, or build, a single-node engine. This
is the site's default order through the first four parts, plus the relational and embedded case
studies that tie the mechanisms back to real engines. It skips nothing in those parts, so it is
the longest single-node route, and the one every other route borrows from.

**Distributed systems.** For someone working on replication, sharding, consensus or distributed
transactions. It takes a deliberately thin slice of single-node material — the anatomy of an
engine, transactions and isolation, application access — then all of transactions and recovery,
then the distributed parts. Look at how many prerequisites it pulls in beyond what it names: that
gap is the single-node knowledge distributed designs quietly depend on. The natural stopping point
is after replication and consensus; the distributed-transaction and architecture material after
it is where specialists go.

## Goals are not modules

The same dropdown offers goals — *make this query fast*, *survive a crash without losing data*,
*design a sharded store*, *stop a race condition in my app*. Pick one and notice that a goal is
never just its own module. "Make this query fast" names two modules and lights many more, because
tuning a plan assumes you can read one, and reading one assumes you know what the operators do and
what they cost.

Then use **Then also learn…** to stack a second goal on the first. The modules marked **+** are
what the second goal adds *beyond* what you already needed. Stacking two goals from the same
region of the curriculum adds little; stacking a query-tuning goal with a sharding goal adds a
whole second foundation, because sharding leans on replication, and replication leans on
transactions and the write-ahead log.

## Catching a route that lies

Hand-written reading lists are often out of order in a way that is easy to miss: they put an
exciting module first and circle back to its foundations later. The explorer checks for exactly
that. Select **Distributed-first (a common mistake)** — a route that starts with replication and
consensus, then turns to transactions and logging. The replication and consensus chips are marked
**⚠**, and the note names the prerequisites they are listed ahead of: transactions and isolation,
and write-ahead logging. Read in that order, the replication pages would lean on material the route
only reaches afterwards.

A prerequisite that a route never mentions is *not* flagged — it is simply pulled in and read first.
The only thing flagged is a route that names both a module and its prerequisite, in the wrong order.

:::tip[Use the table]
Open **Show the numbers** under the map. It breaks the selected route into legs and shows, for each
leg, how many modules and pages it adds on top of the legs before it and the running time. That is
the honest answer to "how long will this take": a leg that looks short can be expensive because of
the prerequisites it is the first to need.
:::

## Where the time estimates come from

Every hour figure on these pages comes from one stated assumption — a fixed number of minutes per
page, covering reading one of these pages and spending time with its simulator — multiplied by the
page count the curriculum data reports. It is a planning number, not a promise. Skimming pages you
already know is expected; the estimate is useful mainly for comparing routes with each other.

:::note[What to take forward]
A route is only as good as its prerequisites. Pick the route or goal that matches what you need,
trust the modules it pulls in over the ones you would have guessed, and stop at the stopping point
unless you are going to own that layer.
:::
