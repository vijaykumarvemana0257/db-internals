Every module on this site carries two kinds of label, and they answer different questions. A
module's **level** says where it sits in the curriculum. A page's **depth** says how far into the
mechanism that page goes. Confusing the two is how people either give up on a module too early or
spend an afternoon on material they did not need.

## Module levels

Modules are tagged with one of four levels, in order:

- **Foundations** — assumes nothing beyond general programming. The vocabulary everything else
  uses: relations and keys, what a database engine is made of, what the hardware and operating
  system actually promise.
- **Core** — the mechanisms every engineer who works with databases should be able to reason
  about: pages, the buffer pool, B-trees, LSM trees, indexes.
- **Advanced** — mechanisms you need when you own a system's behavior under load or failure:
  concurrency control, recovery, cost-based optimization, buffer-pool contention, latch-free
  index structures.
- **Expert** — the material specialists and people building data systems need: consensus
  variants, distributed transaction protocols.

Level is a statement about *prerequisites*, not difficulty for its own sake. An expert module is
expert because it stands on a long chain of earlier ones.

## Page depth

Inside a module, each page is marked **basics**, **intermediate** or **deep**. The strip under
the chain shows the selected module's pages in reading order, colored by depth. The default,
B-Trees, is a clean example: it opens with basics — structure, search, splits — moves through
intermediate pages on crash safety, fill factor, bulk loading and key layout, and ends on deep
pages about collation and the external-memory bounds behind why fanout matters.

The two labels vary independently. A *core* module can end on *deep* pages, and an *advanced*
module can open on a *basics* page. Pick a few modules and compare the level in the stats with the
depth mix in the note.

## Where a module is enough

The stat labelled **Enough for most engineers** marks the last page before the first deep one.
Everything up to that point is the mechanism and its practical consequences; the deep pages are
where a module goes into implementation detail, theory, or the edge cases that matter mainly to
people building or deeply tuning that component. For B-Trees, the marker falls just before the
collation page.

That example is also a warning. The collation page is marked deep, but it is where you learn why a
`LIKE 'prefix%'` query can or cannot use an ordinary text index — something an application engineer
tuning queries will hit. So read the marker as *a place to pause and decide*, not a wall: glance at
the titles of the deep pages and read any that name a problem you actually have.

This marker is derived from the page depths, so it is only as good as their ordering. A module
that opens on a deep page is labelled *starts deep* — it is a specialist module as a whole, and
the useful question is whether you need the module at all, not where to stop inside it. And where a
module places an intermediate page after a deep one, that later page is past the marker even though
it is not deep — the depth strip makes those visible at a glance.

Routes have their own stopping points, described on the
[routes page](../../../p01-foundations/start-here-routes-and-method/01-three-routes-through-the-same-modules/).
Those say which *modules* a role can stop before; this marker says which *pages* inside a module.

## Reading the chain

The columns lay out everything the selected module assumes. The leftmost column is the module
itself, the next holds what it declares directly, and each column further right holds prerequisites
one step further back — placed by the *longest* path, so a module that is needed both directly and
through something else appears in the column that respects both.

Each prerequisite is declared by the module that needs it, in the curriculum data this site is
built from. That makes the chain a check on everything else: a reading route that disagrees with
the chain is wrong, and it shows up here as a module whose foundations are missing.

## When skipping a prerequisite is safe

Use **Skip a prerequisite** to remove one module from the chain. Every module that transitively
depends on it is marked **⚠**, and the note counts how many more depend on it elsewhere in the
curriculum.

The size of that blast radius is the answer to "can I skip this?". On the default chain, skipping
the Buffer Pool strands only B-Trees itself: the buffer pool is a direct prerequisite with nothing
else in this chain built on it, so if you already understand frames, pins and eviction, skipping
it costs you little. Now skip the Data Models and SQL Primer instead, and every other module in the
chain is marked, because the hardware, anatomy, page-layout and buffer-pool modules all assume its
vocabulary. That is the general rule:

- **Safe to skip** when the module has a small blast radius *and* you already know its content —
  for example, you have worked with the mechanism in production.
- **Not safe to skip** when it sits near the root of many chains. Foundations modules almost always
  do, which is why they are cheap to read and expensive to miss.

A stranded page still reads. It does not break; it just leans on terms and mechanisms it assumes
you already have, and the failure mode is quiet — you follow the words without being able to
predict what the mechanism will do.

## Recommended later reading

A few modules point forward to modules later in the curriculum as *recommended later reading*
rather than as prerequisites — for example, Index Selection points ahead to cost-based
optimization and EXPLAIN. Those modules are written to stand on their own, introducing just
enough of the later material to make their decisions, and they tell you where the full treatment
lives. Select one and the note lists its forward pointers. They never appear in the chain, because
you do not need them first.

:::note[What to take forward]
Level tells you where a module sits; depth tells you how far a page goes. Stop a module at its last
page before deep unless you are going to own that component, and before skipping a prerequisite,
check how much of the chain stands on it.
:::
