The [three engineering routes](../../../p01-foundations/start-here-routes-and-method/01-three-routes-through-the-same-modules/)
are organized around what you build. The three routes on this page are organized around what you
*run*: keeping a database up, moving data out of it into analytical systems, and operating a fleet
of databases for other teams. They use the same modules and the same prerequisite graph, and they
come with a second kind of entry point — a place to land when something is already on fire.

The explorer here lists the three role routes first and the engineering routes after them, so you
can switch between, say, **SRE / DBA on call** and **Application engineer** and watch how much of
the map they share.

## SRE / DBA on call

The SRE route follows the order in which things break a production database. It starts with the
anatomy of an engine, then MVCC storage and vacuum — because long transactions, bloat and
transaction-ID horizons are behind a large share of real incidents — then write-ahead logging and
crash recovery, then replication and its operations, and finally the operating modules on
observability, failover and poolers, backups, and incident response.

The order is not arbitrary, and the explorer shows why. Write-ahead logging is listed after MVCC
storage because the WAL module declares MVCC storage as a prerequisite: you cannot reason about
what recovery replays until you know what a tuple version is. If you move those legs around in
your head, the validator on the previous page is what would catch it.

A reasonable stopping point is after the replication leg. Everything up to there is the mental
model you need to *diagnose*; the operating leg is the tooling and procedure you need to *act*.

**Sideways entry.** If you are paged, do not start at leg one. The triage playbooks in Part 12 are
written to be entered from a symptom — replication lag, a disk filling up, a vacuum that cannot
advance, a sudden connection storm — and they link back into the modules that explain each
mechanism. Use the route for learning and the playbooks for incidents.

## Data engineer

The data-engineer route runs from data models to columnar storage, then analytical engines and
lakehouse table formats, then logs and change data capture, then warehouse pipelines and how
analytics are delivered.

Select it and look at Parts 4 and 7 before you read further, because the most useful thing this
route teaches is visible in what it pulls in. It *names* no transaction or replication modules,
and a data engineer could reasonably expect to skip that material. The graph disagrees. Change
data capture reads a database's replication log; a log-structured pipeline inherits partitioning,
ordering and delivery guarantees from the systems underneath it; exactly-once claims are
consensus and transaction claims in disguise. So a large share of both parts lights up as
prerequisites. A pipeline that silently drops or duplicates rows during a failover is usually
built by someone who took the shortcut the route *looks* like it offers.

The natural stopping point is after logs and CDC. Warehouse pipelines and delivery semantics are
where you go once you own the pipeline end to end.

**Sideways entry.** The decision guides and comparison matrices in Part 12 are the pages to open
when the question is "which system should this data live in" rather than "how does this system
work".

## Platform engineer

The platform-engineer route is for the team that provisions, secures and upgrades databases that
other teams depend on. It starts with provisioning and fleet lifecycle, then security, identity
and tenant isolation, then observability and capacity, and ends with managed services, cost and
upgrades.

Observability and capacity come before managed services for the same reason as on the SRE route:
the module on running databases on managed platforms declares the observability module as a
prerequisite. Choosing a managed service well means knowing which signals you will lose visibility
into and which capacity limits become someone else's knob.

This route overlaps heavily with the application-engineer route — switch between the two and most
of the lit foundation is the same. The platform engineer needs the application engineer's model of
connections, transactions and schema change, because those are exactly the things a platform has
to make safe at scale. A reasonable stopping point is after observability and capacity.

**Sideways entry.** The lifecycle checklists and review gates in Part 12 are written for the moment
a new database is about to take real traffic, or a major upgrade is scheduled: they list the
evidence a system should produce before that happens.

## The shared core

Switch through all six routes and watch which chips stay lit. The anatomy of an engine,
transactions and isolation, and the core storage and query modules are on nearly every route.
That shared core is the part of the site worth reading carefully regardless of your role — the
role-specific modules sit on top of it, and they are much faster to read once it is in place.

:::note[What to take forward]
Pick the route for the job you do, but read the prerequisites it pulls in rather than the ones you
expected — for data engineers especially, the modules the route did not name are where pipelines
break. When something is already broken, enter from the Part 12 playbooks instead of from the
start of a route.
:::
