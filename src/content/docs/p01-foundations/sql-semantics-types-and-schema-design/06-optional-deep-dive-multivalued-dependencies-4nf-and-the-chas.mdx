BCNF removes every anomaly a functional dependency can cause and then stops, because an FD is
a statement about single values and the worst redundancy in a schema is usually a statement
about *sets*. This page is the theory behind that stopping point. It is genuinely skippable on
a first pass; if you skip it, take the one-line translation with you — **two independent
one-to-many facts about the same entity must not share a table**, and the symptom that you
broke that rule is an update whose row count is a product.

## The dependency BCNF cannot see

`employee(emp, skill, phone)`. An engineer has several skills. She has several contact numbers.
The two lists have nothing to do with each other. Look for a functional dependency to violate
and you find none: `emp` does not determine `skill`, `skill` does not determine `phone`, and the
only candidate key is all three columns. Every FD that holds is trivial, so the table is in
BCNF — and it is obviously, expensively wrong.

What holds instead is a **multivalued dependency**. Writing `Z` for the attributes outside `X`
and `Y`, the MVD `X ↠ Y` holds in `R` when, for every pair of tuples `t` and `u` with
`t[X] = u[X]`, the relation *also contains* the tuple that agrees with `t` on `X` and `Y` and
with `u` on `Z`. Read operationally: once you fix `X`, the set of `Y`-values and the set of
`Z`-values are independent, so the relation is obliged to hold their cross product for that `X`.

Four properties make MVDs behave differently from FDs, and all four matter in practice:

- **Complementation.** `X ↠ Y` holds if and only if `X ↠ R − X − Y` holds. You never get one
  without the other, which is why saying "`emp ↠ skill`" is the whole assertion — `emp ↠ phone`
  comes free. FDs have no such symmetry.
- **Every FD is an MVD.** `X → Y` implies `X ↠ Y`. MVDs are the weaker, more general statement,
  and that is exactly why they survive a BCNF decomposition.
- **Trivial cases.** `X ↠ Y` is trivial when `Y ⊆ X` or when `X ∪ Y` is all of `R`. A
  two-attribute relation therefore cannot violate 4NF: there is nothing left over to be
  independent of.
- **It is a tuple-generating constraint.** An FD forbids states. An MVD *requires* tuples. That
  is the whole cost model: assert the MVD and the table must physically store every combination.

The inference rules (Armstrong's three, plus complementation, MVD augmentation and transitivity,
plus replication and coalescence tying FDs and MVDs together) were proved sound and complete by
Beeri, Fagin and Howard in 1977. You will almost never run them by hand — the chase below does
the same job mechanically — but the *shape* is worth knowing: MVD reasoning is not FD reasoning
with extra arrows.

Toggle the MVD off in the lab and the table stops being redundant and starts being *wrong* in a
different way: the projections onto `(emp, skill)` and `(emp, phone)` rejoin to strictly more
rows than you stored. Those extra rows are not a bug in the join. They are the definition: a
relation that does not satisfy the MVD cannot be reassembled from those two projections.

## 4NF, and exactly what it buys

`R` is in **fourth normal form** with respect to a set of dependencies if, for every non-trivial
MVD `X ↠ Y` that holds on `R`, `X` is a superkey of `R`. `employee` fails on the only test that
matters: `emp ↠ skill` is non-trivial and `emp` is not a superkey. The decomposition algorithm is
the same shape as the BCNF one — find a violating `X ↠ Y`, replace `R` with `X ∪ Y` and
`X ∪ (R − X − Y)`, recurse — and it lands on `employee_skill(emp, skill)` and
`employee_phone(emp, phone)`. Because every FD is an MVD, a schema in 4NF is automatically in
BCNF.

The theorem underneath is **Fagin's**, from the 1977 TODS paper that introduced 4NF: a binary
decomposition of `R` into `R₁` and `R₂` is lossless **if and only if** `R₁ ∩ R₂ ↠ R₁ − R₂` holds
in `R`. The "if" is the useful half at a whiteboard; the "only if" is the half that changes how
you think. Losslessness is not a property of picking sensible columns, and it is not something
you verify by staring at today's data — it is precisely a multivalued dependency on the shared
columns. The familiar FD test ("the shared columns must be a key of one side") is just the
corollary you get because every FD is an MVD.

| Normal form | Constrains | Rule | What a decomposition guarantees |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3NF | FDs | every non-trivial `X → A` has `X` a superkey, or `A` prime | lossless **and** dependency-preserving, always achievable |
| BCNF | FDs | every non-trivial `X → A` has `X` a superkey | lossless always; may lose an FD you can no longer check locally |
| 4NF | MVDs | every non-trivial `X ↠ Y` has `X` a superkey | lossless always (Fagin); preservation still not guaranteed |
| 5NF / PJNF | join dependencies | every non-trivial JD is implied by the candidate keys | may require an *n*-ary projection; no binary split suffices |

The arithmetic is the reason to care. With `s` skills and `p` phone numbers you store `s × p`
rows to record `s + p` facts. Six skills and four numbers is 24 rows for 10 facts; across
5 000 employees with those averages it is 120 000 rows instead of 50 000, and every one of the
extra 70 000 exists only so that a `DISTINCT`-free `COUNT` can lie to you.

## Finding one in a table you inherited

There is no DDL for this. `CHECK` is evaluated per row and cannot see the rest of the group;
`UNIQUE` constrains a column list, not a cross product; and `CREATE ASSERTION`, the SQL-92
construct that could express a multi-row invariant, is not implemented by PostgreSQL, MySQL or
SQL Server. Your options are a trigger that re-validates the whole `X`-group on every insert and
delete, or decomposition. Only one of them also makes the update cheap.

Diagnosis is a single aggregate. If the stored row count for a group equals the product of the
distinct counts of two other columns, that group is a materialized cross product:

```sql
SELECT emp,
       count(*)                                      AS rows_stored,
       count(DISTINCT skill)                         AS skills,
       count(DISTINCT phone)                         AS phones,
       count(DISTINCT skill) * count(DISTINCT phone) AS product
FROM employee
GROUP BY emp
HAVING count(DISTINCT skill) > 1
   AND count(DISTINCT phone) > 1;
```

Both possible answers are bad news, in different ways. Where `rows_stored = product`, the MVD
holds and you are paying for it on every write. Where `rows_stored < product`, the MVD does
*not* hold, which means the table is already recording which skill goes with which phone number
— a claim nobody intended to make, and one that silently breaks any query that projects the two
columns apart and rejoins them.

## Join dependencies, and the relation no binary split fixes

4NF is not the end of the ladder, because a relation can be decomposable into three projections
while every pairwise split loses information. The standard example is real enough to keep:
`shipment(supplier, part, project)`, under the business rule that if a supplier supplies a part,
that part is used by a project, and the supplier already works on that project, then the supplier
supplies that part *to* that project. That rule is a **join dependency**, written
`⋈[(supplier,part), (part,project), (project,supplier)]`: the relation must equal the natural
join of its three projections.

Take the instance `(s1,p1,j2)`, `(s1,p2,j1)`, `(s2,p1,j1)`. The rule fires — `s1` supplies `p1`,
`p1` is used by `j1`, `s1` works on `j1` — so `(s1,p1,j1)` must also be present, giving four rows.
Now try the binary split into `(supplier,part)` and `(part,project)`: rejoining those two
projections yields five rows, including `(s2,p1,j2)`, which nobody asserted. Every other binary
split fails the same way. Only the three-way projection reconstructs the relation exactly.

An MVD is precisely a **binary** join dependency: `X ↠ Y` is the same statement as
`⋈[X∪Y, X∪Z]`. **5NF** (also called projection-join normal form; Fagin, 1979) generalizes the
rule one more step: every non-trivial join dependency that holds on the relation must be implied
by its candidate keys. A relation can sit in 4NF, carry a genuine ternary JD, and still be
decomposable — which is why 5NF cannot be reached by repeatedly cutting relations in half.

:::caution[5NF is a claim about the world, not a refactor]
The three-way split is only correct while the cyclic rule is true. The moment reality admits a
supplier who works on a project and supplies a part *but not that part to that project*, the
decomposed schema starts manufacturing shipments that do not exist. Genuine non-key join
dependencies are rare, and splitting on a rule you have not verified is worse than the
redundancy you were trying to remove.
:::

## The chase

The chase is the decision procedure that answers all of this mechanically. To test whether the
decomposition of `R` into `R₁ … Rₙ` is lossless under a set of dependencies, build a **tableau**:
one row per fragment, holding the distinguished symbol `aₖ` in every column that fragment keeps
and a fresh placeholder `bᵢₖ` in every column it drops. Then apply the dependencies as rewrite
rules until nothing changes:

| Dependency | Rule |
|---|---|
| FD `X → Y` | two rows agreeing on `X` must agree on `Y`: equate the two symbols everywhere in the tableau — a distinguished `a` always beats a placeholder `b` |
| MVD `X ↠ Y` | two rows agreeing on `X`: add the row that takes `Y` from the first and the remaining columns from the second |
| JD `⋈[R₁ … Rₙ]` | rows `t₁ … tₙ` that agree on every shared column: add the row that takes each `Rᵢ`'s columns from `tᵢ` |

The decomposition is lossless **iff** some row becomes all-`a`. That is the entire test, and the
stepper above runs it: an FD kills a placeholder, an MVD or a JD adds a row, and the verdict is
whether the all-`a` row ever appears. Switch between the two FD scenarios to see the point that
catches people — the same decomposition of the same three columns is lossless under
`order_id → customer` and lossy under `item → customer`, because only a dependency rooted at the
*intersection* can ever fire.

Termination is not an accident. FDs, MVDs and JDs are **full** dependencies: they never invent a
value that was not already in the tableau. So the symbol alphabet is fixed, the number of
distinct rows over that alphabet is finite, and every step either merges two symbols or adds a
row that was not there — both bounded. The chase always halts, though it can take exponentially
many steps in the size of the dependency set.

A stuck tableau is not a non-answer; it is a **counterexample**. Read the final rows as a
relation over the symbols and you have an instance that satisfies every dependency in the set and
whose projections rejoin to strictly more tuples than it contains. That is the lossy case,
constructed for you.

The same machinery answers more than losslessness:

- **Dependency implication.** To test whether a set `D` implies `X → Y`, start from a two-row
  tableau that agrees on `X` and differs everywhere else, chase under `D`, and check whether the
  `Y` columns were forced equal. The MVD version starts the same way and checks whether the
  completion row appears. This is the algorithm from Maier, Mendelzon and Sagiv's 1979 TODS paper
  that gave the technique its name.
- **Query containment and equivalence** for conjunctive queries under a set of dependencies,
  via the tableau formulation of Aho, Sagiv and Ullman.
- **Query optimization.** Real planners run degenerate chases constantly. PostgreSQL's join
  removal drops a `LEFT JOIN` whose inner relation is provably unique on the join key and whose
  columns are unreferenced — one chase step over a key dependency, and the reason an ORM's
  twelve-table view often plans as four. Materialized-view rewriting and semantic query
  optimization are the same idea with a larger dependency set.

The boundary is worth knowing: everything above holds for full dependencies. Allow *embedded*
dependencies — an MVD asserted only on a projection of the relation — and implication becomes
undecidable (Herrmann, 1995). Allow existential tuple-generating dependencies, as data exchange
does, and the chase may not terminate at all, which is why that literature is full of acyclicity
conditions designed to guarantee it does.

## Where it bites

The production symptom is arithmetic, not theory: **an update whose row count is a product**.
`UPDATE employee SET phone = $2 WHERE emp = $1 AND phone = $3` reporting 24 rows for one changed
phone number is an MVD announcing itself. Three consequences follow, and none of them are about
elegance:

- **Write amplification through MVCC.** Twenty-four logical duplicates are twenty-four physical
  row versions. PostgreSQL writes a new heap tuple per row plus an index entry in every index on
  a changed column — HOT applies only when no indexed column changed *and* the page has room —
  and `autovacuum` must later reclaim twenty-four dead tuples. InnoDB writes twenty-four undo
  records, updates every affected secondary index, and holds twenty-four row locks for the
  duration of the transaction, which is twenty-four times the deadlock surface.
- **The constraint is atomic or it is broken.** All twenty-four rows must change in one
  transaction. Update twenty-three and the MVD no longer holds; from that moment any query that
  projects the columns apart and rejoins them — including a perfectly innocent reporting view —
  produces rows that were never asserted.
- **Fan-out double counting.** `SUM(salary)` over the flat table multiplies every salary by that
  employee's phone-number count. This is the same fan-out trap as
  [page 1](/p01-foundations/sql-semantics-types-and-schema-design/01-sql-semantics-engineers-get-wrong/),
  with the cross product baked into storage rather than created by a join, and `DISTINCT` will
  not save you because the duplicated values are genuinely distinct rows. BI tools have a name
  for the modelling version — the *chasm trap*, two independent one-to-many bridges hanging off
  one dimension — and the only correct fix is two queries, not one join.

It is not a relational-only problem, because it is not about tables. Two independent arrays in
one document are an MVD in document form: MongoDB stores them cheaply and then makes you pay on
read, since `$unwind` on both produces the cross product, and a compound index can include at
most one array-valued field per document. BigQuery's `REPEATED` fields behave identically —
`UNNEST` two of them in the same `FROM` clause and you have cross-joined them. In PostgreSQL,
two `jsonb_array_elements` calls in the `FROM` list are an implicit lateral cross join over the
same row. And ORMs enforce the rule by refusing: Hibernate's `MultipleBagFetchException` exists
precisely because eagerly fetching two `List` collections in one query produces a Cartesian
product it cannot deduplicate.

## Where to stop

4NF is usually free. If you drew the ER model and gave each independent one-to-many relationship
its own table, you were already there; violations arrive later, by accident — a "one big table"
export, a denormalized read model, an EAV design, a JSON document flattened into rows for a
warehouse. 5NF is a different kind of decision: it asks you to assert a cyclic business rule is
universally true, and it is worth the three-way split only when it is. Fagin's later
domain-key normal form is a beautiful result and not an engineering target.

The practical payoff of all of this returns in Part 5, where the same question — which facts are
independent, and which storage engine's data model can express that independence natively — is
what decides whether a document, wide-column or relational store is the right home for a given
entity. See the [curriculum map](/curriculum/) for that module.

:::note[What to take forward]
An MVD is the constraint that forces a relation to store a cross product, and BCNF cannot see it
because no FD is violated. 4NF says every non-trivial MVD's determinant must be a superkey, and
Fagin's theorem makes that the exact condition for a binary decomposition to be lossless. The
chase decides losslessness and dependency implication mechanically, and hands you a
counterexample when the answer is no. In engineering terms: independent one-to-many facts belong
in separate tables, separate arrays or separate documents, and the symptom that you got it wrong
is a single logical update that rewrites a cross product of rows.
:::
