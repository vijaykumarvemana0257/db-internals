A schema that stores the same fact twice has turned a one-row `UPDATE` into a scan-and-rewrite,
and has created a state — the two copies disagreeing — that no constraint can forbid.
Normalization is the mechanical procedure for finding those duplicated facts before they are in
production: you write down the dependencies you are actually asserting, and an algorithm tells
you where the schema stores one of them more than once.

## A functional dependency is an assertion, not an observation

`X → Y` holds on relation `R` if **every legal instance** of `R` has the property that two
tuples agreeing on `X` agree on `Y`. That word *legal* is the whole difficulty. Today's rows can
only ever **refute** a dependency; they can never confirm one. If no customer in your table
shares an email address, that does not mean `email → cust_id` — it means nobody has tried yet.
Dependencies come from the business, and getting them wrong in either direction is expensive:
assert one that isn't true and you will normalize away a column you need, miss one that is true
and you will store it redundantly forever.

Armstrong gave the complete inference system for dependencies in 1974, and it is three rules:
**reflexivity** (`Y ⊆ X` implies `X → Y`), **augmentation** (`X → Y` implies `XZ → YZ`), and
**transitivity** (`X → Y` and `Y → Z` implies `X → Z`). Sound and complete means everything
implied by `F` is derivable from those three, and nothing else is. The convenience rules you
actually use — union (`X → Y`, `X → Z` gives `X → YZ`), decomposition, pseudotransitivity —
are all derived from them.

You never enumerate the closure of the whole dependency set; it is exponential and useless. You
compute **attribute closure** instead: start with `X`, and repeatedly add the right side of any
dependency whose left side you already have, until nothing changes. That single routine answers
every question on this page:

- `X` is a **superkey** iff `X⁺` is all of `R`.
- `X` is a **candidate key** iff it is a superkey and no proper subset of it is.
- An attribute is **prime** iff it appears in some candidate key.
- `X → Y` is implied by `F` iff `Y ⊆ X⁺`.

Click attributes in the lab to build `X` and watch `X⁺` grow. In `order_line`, `{order_id}⁺`
pulls in the customer, the email, the tier, the warehouse and the region — but never `sku` or
`qty`, so it stops short of the relation and is not a superkey. That gap is the schema's whole
problem stated in one line.

## The ladder, and the anomaly each rung removes

| Rung | The rule | What it removes |
|---|---|---|
| 1NF | One value per cell; no repeating groups | Rows you cannot address with a `WHERE` clause |
| 2NF | No non-prime attribute depends on a *proper subset* of a candidate key | Per-order facts stored once per order *line* |
| 3NF | No non-prime attribute depends on a non-key attribute (no transitive dependency) | Per-warehouse facts stored once per order |
| BCNF | *Every* non-trivial determinant is a superkey | The residue 3NF forgives when candidate keys overlap |

**1NF** is the rung people assume is free and is not. The classical reading — atomic values —
has been muddied by engines that ship non-scalar types deliberately: PostgreSQL arrays,
`jsonb`, composite types, `hstore`. The useful operational test is not "is this value atomic"
but **"does anything ever need to reach inside it?"** A `text[]` of tags that you only ever
render is a perfectly good column. A comma-joined `sku_list` that a report has to split, unnest,
`GROUP BY` and — the giveaway — enforce a foreign key against is a 1NF violation with a GIN
index taped over the top; it cannot be constrained, cannot be joined efficiently, and every
consumer reimplements the parser.

**2NF** is about composite keys only. `order_line`'s key is `{order_id, sku}`, but `order_id`
alone determines the customer and the warehouse, so those columns repeat on every line of a
multi-line order. Switch the lab to 2NF and watch the partial dependency get lifted into its own
relation, leaving `{order_id, sku, qty}` behind.

**3NF** catches the next layer: `order_id → whse → region`. `region` depends on the key only
because it depends on something else that does. Nothing about the key of `order_line` changes;
the fact is still stored once per line.

**BCNF** tightens 3NF's escape clause. 3NF forgives `X → A` when `A` is prime — when the
dependent attribute belongs to some candidate key. That exemption only matters when candidate
keys overlap, which is exactly the `address` preset: `{street, city, state}` and `{street, zip}`
are both candidate keys, every attribute is prime, so `zip → city` is *legal in 3NF* while the
city name is still stored once per street address in that ZIP. Load the address schema, leave it
flat, and the lab shows it sitting in 3NF with redundant cells shaded.

Every rung removes the same three anomalies in different guises. In flat `order_line`:

- **Update anomaly** — customer `c1`'s email lives in four rows. One `UPDATE` has to hit all
  four *atomically*; a `WHERE` clause that misses one leaves the database holding two emails for
  one customer, and no `CHECK`, `UNIQUE` or foreign key can express that this is wrong.
- **Insertion anomaly** — you cannot record a customer who has not ordered, because `order_id`
  and `sku` are the primary key and cannot be `NULL`.
- **Deletion anomaly** — delete the two lines of order `o-1003`, the only order customer `c2`
  has ever placed, and `c2` disappears from the database entirely: their email and their tier
  were only ever recorded as a side effect of having ordered something.

## A decomposition has to prove two things

Splitting a relation is not automatically safe. There are exactly two obligations.

**Lossless join.** A binary decomposition of `R` into `R₁` and `R₂` is lossless when the shared
attributes determine one of the halves: `R₁ ∩ R₂ → R₁` or `R₁ ∩ R₂ → R₂` (Heath, 1971).
Otherwise the natural join of the two parts contains rows that were never in `R` — *spurious
tuples* — and you have silently lost information by adding rows. Switch the lab to **naive** and
slide the split point: it cuts the relation at an arbitrary column, joins the fragments back
together, and shows you the invented rows marked `✕`. The check is not asserted, it is run: the
fragments are projected from the instance and natural-joined, and the result is diffed against
the original rows.

This is why the BCNF algorithm splits where it does. When `X → Y` violates BCNF inside `R`, you
produce `X⁺ ∩ R` and `X ∪ (R − X⁺)`. Those two share exactly `X`, and `X` is a key of the first
by construction, so losslessness is free at every step.

**Dependency preservation.** Each fragment can enforce the dependencies that fit inside it, as a
primary key or a `UNIQUE` constraint. A dependency is *preserved* when it is implied by the union
of the projections `πRᵢ(F)` — which is decided by a fixpoint: grow `Z` from `X` by adding
`(Z ∩ Rᵢ)⁺ ∩ Rᵢ` for each fragment until nothing changes, then ask whether `Y ⊆ Z`. If it is
not, that constraint can no longer be checked without a join.

The two obligations do not always both survive:

| Algorithm | Lossless | Dependency-preserving | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3NF synthesis (Bernstein, 1976) — minimal cover, one relation per determinant, plus a candidate key | always | always | 3NF |
| BCNF decomposition — recursive split on a violating determinant | always | **not always** | BCNF |

Run the address schema to see the gap concretely. 3NF synthesis hands the relation back
unchanged, because it is already in 3NF. BCNF splits it into `{zip, city, state}` and
`{street, zip}` — and `{street, city, state} → zip` is now enforceable by nothing: the two
attributes it constrains live in different tables. There is no dependency-preserving BCNF
decomposition of that relation. Not "the algorithm doesn't find one" — none exists.

In practice you pick one of three exits, and the right one is usually the first:

1. **Stay in 3NF and declare both candidate keys.** Keep `address(street, city, state, zip)`
   with `UNIQUE (street, city, state)` and `UNIQUE (street, zip)`; both dependencies are now
   enforced by the engine, and you accept that `zip → city, state` repeats. This is the normal
   answer.
2. **Go to BCNF and re-enforce the lost dependency with machinery** — a trigger, a deferred
   constraint, or a materialized helper table with a unique index. You have now reintroduced
   write-time work to remove redundancy; sometimes worth it, rarely.
3. **Discover the dependency was never true.** A good share of BCNF conflicts dissolve when
   someone checks the claim against reality: ZIP codes do straddle city lines, and instructors do
   teach two sections.

## When to stop: BCNF plus judgement

BCNF is the stopping point for almost every production schema. Beyond it lies 4NF and the theory
of multivalued dependencies, and those matter for a specific, recognizable shape — two
independent multi-valued facts crammed into one table — which the
[optional next page](../../../p01-foundations/sql-semantics-types-and-schema-design/06-optional-deep-dive-multivalued-dependencies-4nf-and-the-chas/)
covers. You can skip it and lose nothing on a first pass.

What you should *not* do is treat denormalization as a modeling decision. It is a physical,
read-versus-write trade, and the discipline is that **every duplicated value must have a
mechanism that maintains it**. A copied column with no maintainer is a cache with no
invalidation story, and it will drift — usually first noticed as a report that disagrees with
the detail page.

The mechanisms that qualify:

| Mechanism | Maintained by | Staleness |
|---|---|---|
| Covering index (`INCLUDE` columns in PostgreSQL/SQL Server) | The engine, transactionally | None — it is not a second copy of the fact, it is a second copy of the bytes |
| Generated column (PostgreSQL `STORED` since 12, MySQL virtual/stored since 5.7) | The engine, on write | None |
| Indexed view (SQL Server), materialized view with `REFRESH FAST ON COMMIT` (Oracle) | The engine, synchronously | None, at a write cost |
| `MATERIALIZED VIEW` (PostgreSQL) | You, via `REFRESH MATERIALIZED VIEW [CONCURRENTLY]` | Until the next refresh; `CONCURRENTLY` needs a unique index on the view |
| Counter/summary table | Your triggers or your writer | Whatever your code guarantees |
| Star-schema dimension (OLAP) | The load job | One load cycle — and deliberately so |

The cost of the copy is not the disk. In PostgreSQL, an `UPDATE` that touches four rows writes
four new tuple versions, leaves four dead ones for autovacuum, writes WAL for all of it, and —
unless the update is HOT-eligible (no indexed column changed, and free space on the same page,
which is why `FILLFACTOR` below the default 100 exists) — inserts new entries into every index
on the table. The lab's "rows rewritten" stat is the leading indicator for all of that: flat
`order_line` rewrites 6 rows to re-home one warehouse; the decomposed schema rewrites 1.

## Choosing the key, which is also a storage decision

In a clustered engine the primary key is not only an identity — it is the physical sort order of
the table and the row locator in every secondary index. InnoDB *is* its primary-key B+tree, and
every secondary index entry stores the full primary key as its pointer back to the row. Choose a
16-byte random key and you have widened every secondary index on the table by 16 bytes per entry
and randomized every insert.

| Key | Bytes | Insert locality | Use when |
|---|---|---|---|
| `bigint` identity / `bigserial` | 8 | Perfect — monotonic, always the right-edge leaf | Default. Use `GENERATED BY DEFAULT AS IDENTITY` over `serial`; it is the standard spelling and owns its sequence properly |
| UUIDv4 | 16 (`uuid`; never `text`) | Worst case — every insert lands in a random leaf | Identities minted by many clients that must not coordinate, and you accept the index cost |
| UUIDv7 / ULID | 16 | Near-sequential: a 48-bit big-endian millisecond prefix | You need client-minted, globally unique *and* index-friendly keys |
| Natural composite | Varies | Follows the natural key | Junction tables where the pair genuinely *is* the identity — `(order_id, sku)` |

Random UUIDs are the expensive one, and the mechanism is worth being precise about. Inserts
scatter across the whole B-tree, so the index's hot set is the *entire index* rather than its
right edge; once that exceeds `shared_buffers` or `innodb_buffer_pool_size`, every insert becomes
a read plus a write of a different page. Page splits happen at random interior points, leaving
leaves around half full instead of nearly full. PostgreSQL softens one half of this — the heap is
not clustered, so table inserts still append at the end and only the indexes suffer — while
InnoDB, whose table *is* the clustered index, takes the full hit. Storing a UUID as 36-character
text instead of the 16-byte `uuid` type (or MySQL `BINARY(16)` via `UUID_TO_BIN`) more than
doubles it again.

UUIDv7, standardized in RFC 9562 in 2024, fixes the locality without giving up client-side
minting: the first 48 bits are a big-endian Unix millisecond timestamp, so new keys sort at the
right edge like a sequence. PostgreSQL 18 ships `uuidv7()` as a built-in; before that you used
the `pg_uuidv7` extension or generated it in the application. ULID is the same construction with
a 26-character Crockford base32 text form. MySQL's older trick, `UUID_TO_BIN(uuid, 1)`, swaps the
time fields of a v1 UUID to the same end. Two honest costs: a time-ordered key **leaks creation
time** to anyone who can see it, and sequential keys concentrate every inserter on the same
right-edge leaf page, which becomes a latch hotspot at high insert rates — the classic fixes are
hash partitioning or, in Oracle, a reverse-key index.

Natural keys deserve one rule. A natural key that can **change** is a fan-out update waiting to
happen: every referencing row in every table has to change with it, `ON UPDATE CASCADE` or not,
which is precisely the anomaly normalization was supposed to eliminate. Email addresses,
usernames, ISBNs, tax IDs and SKUs all change in the real world. The discipline: declare the
natural key as a `UNIQUE` constraint so the engine enforces it, and make an immutable surrogate
the primary key that foreign keys point at. Composite natural keys inside junction tables are the
exception — `(order_id, sku)` is not going to be re-issued — and there the composite key also
gives you the clustering you want for free.

## Where it bites in production

- **The one-statement incident.** A single `UPDATE customers SET tier = ...` against a
  denormalized `order_line` rewrites hundreds of thousands of rows, holds row locks on all of
  them, produces an equivalent volume of WAL, and blocks replication apply behind it. The metric
  that catches it is rows-written per logical change, which the lab's flat-versus-decomposed
  counter is a miniature of.
- **Drift you find in a report.** Two copies of a fact and one maintainer means the copy is
  eventually wrong. If you cannot name the mechanism that maintains a duplicated column, it is
  not denormalization, it is a bug with a schedule.
- **The lost dependency nobody re-enforced.** After a BCNF split, the constraint that used to be
  a `UNIQUE` index lives only in application code, and duplicates show up the first time two
  requests race. If a decomposition loses a dependency, write down *where* it is now enforced
  before you ship it.
- **The insert cliff.** Steady write throughput that falls off a shelf when a UUIDv4-keyed
  index outgrows the buffer pool. The signature is buffer reads per insert climbing while the
  row rate falls and CPU stays flat.
- **Over-normalization is real but rarer.** A screen that needs nine joins is a legitimate
  complaint — but the fix is usually a covering index, a view, or a materialized rollup, not a
  wide table. Denormalize the *read path*, never the source of truth.

:::note[What to take forward]
Write the dependencies down, compute closures to find the candidate keys, and split wherever a
determinant is not a superkey. Every split must be lossless — the shared attributes have to be a
key of one side — and any dependency it costs you has to be re-enforced somewhere you can name.
BCNF plus judgement is enough for nearly every production schema; denormalize afterwards, on
purpose, only with a mechanism that maintains the copy. And pick the primary key knowing it is
also the physical order of a clustered table and the row locator in every secondary index — a
subject Part 2 picks up in [B+tree structure](../../../p02-storage-engines/b-trees-and-variants/01-b-tree-structure-invariants-and-search/)
and [splits under load](../../../p02-storage-engines/b-trees-and-variants/04-fill-factor-fragmentation-and-splits-under-load/).
:::
