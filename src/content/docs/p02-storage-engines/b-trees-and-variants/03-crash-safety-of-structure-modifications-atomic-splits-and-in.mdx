Inserting one key can rewrite four pages: the overflowing leaf, a brand-new sibling, the old
right neighbour whose back-pointer moves, and the parent that needs a downlink. None of those
pages belong to the transaction that triggered the split — they hold other people's committed
keys, and other sessions may already be descending through them. So the two obvious answers
are both wrong: you cannot undo a split when the transaction aborts, and you cannot let a crash
leave three of the four changes on disk. What engines do instead is make the whole structure
modification **atomic with respect to redo, and immune to undo**.

## Why the split is not the transaction's to undo

Rollback in a transactional engine means "remove the effects that were mine". A page split has
no such owner. Three separate things go wrong if you try to reverse it byte for byte:

- **The new page is already public.** The moment the right link on the left page points at it,
  any reader that lands on the left page and finds its key above the high key will walk into
  it. Another writer may already have inserted a key of its own there. Restoring the pre-split
  image of the left page would silently delete that key.
- **The parent and the old sibling already agree with the new shape.** The downlink in the
  parent and the `btpo_prev` on the old right neighbour are single fields in pages shared by
  the whole index. Undoing them is not a local operation.
- **Even alone in the database, the undo would be physical, and the page has moved on.** ARIES
  undo is *page-oriented*: it re-finds the page, applies the inverse of the logged change, and
  logs a compensation record. That works for "I put a 40-byte tuple at slot 7" only while slot
  7 still means what it meant. After a split, half the page's slots are somewhere else.

The resolution is the distinction ARIES calls **physiological logging** — physical to a page,
logical within a page. Redo can always be physical, because redo replays history in order onto
the exact pages it was recorded against. Undo cannot, because it runs later, out of order,
against pages that other transactions have since reorganized. So structure modifications are
made **redo-only**, and the compensation for an aborted insert is *logical*: delete the key,
not the space it caused to be allocated. PostgreSQL does not even do that much at abort time —
the index tuple for the aborted row stays on the page as a dead entry until `VACUUM` reclaims
it. Press **Roll back the inserter** in the lab: key 25 stops being visible, and the split,
the right link and the downlink all stay exactly where they are.

:::note[The invariant to hold on to]
A structure modification changes the *shape* of a shared index, not the *data* of one
transaction. Shape changes are redone after a crash and never undone by an abort. The only
thing an abort removes is the key.
:::

## The atomic unit: one record, or one mini-transaction

If a split can never be undone, then recovery must never be able to apply part of one. Each
engine buys that with a different unit of atomicity.

### PostgreSQL nbtree: one multi-page record, and a deliberate gap

`_bt_split()` allocates the new block, builds both halves, and then — inside a critical
section, with every affected buffer exclusively locked and marked dirty — emits a *single* WAL
record, `XLOG_BTREE_SPLIT_L` or `XLOG_BTREE_SPLIT_R` depending on which half the incoming tuple
landed in. That one record registers three blocks (four when an internal page is splitting and
must also clear a child's flag):

| Registered block | Page | What redo does with it |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | the original page, now the left half | Rebuilt from the pre-split image already on disk plus the logged split point and new item |
| 1 | the new right page | Written in full, `REGBUF_WILL_INIT` — there is no prior image to rebuild from |
| 2 | the old right sibling | Its `btpo_prev` is repointed at the new page |

Because block 0 is reconstructed *from what is already on disk*, redo of a split depends on the
pre-split page being intact — which is what full-page writes after a checkpoint exist to
guarantee. And because block 1 is logged in full, a split is one of the fattest records nbtree
emits; a workload that splits constantly is a workload with a WAL-volume problem, which is the
subject of the fill-factor page.

What is conspicuously *not* in that record is the parent's downlink. nbtree inserts it
afterwards, as a separate record (`XLOG_BTREE_INSERT_UPPER`), via `_bt_insert_parent()` walking
back up the `BTStack` the descent recorded on the way down. In between, the left page carries
the flag `BTP_INCOMPLETE_SPLIT` in its `btpo_flags`, which says exactly one thing: *my right
sibling has no downlink in the parent yet*. The downlink record registers the child page too,
so a single redo step both inserts the downlink and clears the flag.

### InnoDB: the mini-transaction

InnoDB has no equivalent gap, because its unit of atomicity is bigger.
`btr_page_split_and_insert()` runs inside a **mini-transaction** (`mtr_t`): the split, the
sibling fix-up and the parent downlink insertion are all one mtr. Its redo records accumulate
in the mtr's own private buffer and are copied into the global log buffer only at
`mtr_commit()`, as a group terminated by `MLOG_MULTI_REC_END`. Recovery applies a multi-record
group only once it has seen that terminator, so a group truncated by a crash at the tail of the
log is discarded whole. There is no undo for any of it: undo records are written for *row*
operations, never for page management, which is why rolling back a million-row load in InnoDB
removes the rows and leaves the index exactly as tall and as fragmented as the load made it.

The mini-transaction is also the scope that holds the page latches — the concurrency side of
the same object, which the later module on B-tree concurrency picks up.

### The general rule: ARIES nested top actions

Mohan et al.'s 1992 ARIES paper names this pattern a **nested top action**. The transaction
performs the structure modification and logs its records normally; when the action completes,
it writes a dummy compensation log record whose `UndoNxtLSN` points to the LSN *immediately
before* the action began. Rollback follows the `PrevLSN` chain backwards, hits the dummy CLR,
and jumps straight over every record the SMO produced. The records are still ordinary redo
records, so recovery replays them; they are simply unreachable from undo. The action has, in
effect, committed on its own, independently of the transaction that performed it.

| Engine | Atomic unit for a split | Intermediate state visible after a crash? | Undo for the SMO |
|---|---|---|---|
| PostgreSQL nbtree | one `SPLIT_L`/`SPLIT_R` record; downlink is a second record | Yes — flagged `BTP_INCOMPLETE_SPLIT` | None |
| InnoDB | one mini-transaction, `MLOG_MULTI_REC_END` terminated | No | None |
| SQLite | the whole write transaction (rollback journal, or WAL frames) | No — and there is only ever one writer | Whole-transaction, page-image based |
| WiredTiger | none: the WAL carries logical row operations, not page structure | N/A — splits are in-memory and re-derived | N/A; durability comes from the checkpoint |

SQLite gets atomicity for free by having a coarser unit and a single writer: its `balance()`
rewrites whatever pages it needs, and nothing is durable until the transaction commits. At the
other end, WiredTiger never logs page structure at all — splits happen in the in-memory btree,
reconciliation writes new page images, and a checkpoint installs a new root atomically, so
recovery replays logical operations onto the last checkpoint's tree and re-splits as needed.
Both are coherent answers to the same question, and both pay for it elsewhere: SQLite in
concurrency, WiredTiger in recovery work per unit of log.

## What a crash actually leaves behind

Drag the crash point in the lab to just after the split record's flush on the nbtree model.
The tree that comes back has a right page nobody's parent points to. It is not corrupt. Every
key in blk 4 is still reachable, and the reason is a single structural fact: the parent's
separator range that used to cover the left page still covers *both* halves, because the new
separator was never inserted. So a search for 40 descends to blk 2, compares 40 against blk 2's
high key of 30, finds it does not belong on this page, and follows `btpo_next` to blk 4. One
extra page read; correct answer.

That rule — **if the key exceeds this page's high key, move right** — is Lehman-Yao's, and it
was invented for concurrency, not for recovery: it is what lets a reader that descends while
another session is mid-split find its key anyway. The half-finished state a crash leaves is
byte-for-byte the same state a concurrent split leaves for a moment during normal running, so
the same rule covers both. Turn off **Reader follows the right link** in the lab and watch a
perfectly ordinary incomplete split turn into a missing row. The recovery rule and the
concurrency rule are one rule seen from two sides.

What the flag forbids is narrower than you might expect. A read-only descent does nothing about
it. But `_bt_moveright()` called with `forupdate` — which is to say, any descent that is on its
way to insert — sees `P_INCOMPLETE_SPLIT` and is *obliged* to call `_bt_finish_split()` first:
re-find the parent, insert the missing downlink, clear the flag, then proceed. Page deletion
does the same, because `VACUUM` must not delete a page that is the target of an unfinished
split. The repair is charged to whichever backend happens to pass through next, and it is a
handful of buffer operations.

This is not how it always worked. Before PostgreSQL 9.4, nbtree's redo routines tracked
incomplete splits in memory during recovery and fixed them up at the *end* of recovery. That
scheme cannot survive continuous replay: a hot standby is never at the end of recovery, so it
would serve queries against a tree whose pending fix-ups had not run. Moving the record of
"unfinished" onto the page itself, where any descent can see it, made the intermediate state a
legal, self-describing state of the index rather than a temporary embarrassment of the recovery
code.

:::caution[The flag is not optional bookkeeping]
Turn off `_bt_finish_split` repair in the lab and run inserts. Searches still work forever, one
extra right-link hop at a time — but the flagged page can never be split again and can never be
deleted, so the first insert that needs to split blk 2 has nowhere to go. A missing downlink
that nothing records and nothing repairs (the "one record per page" model) is worse still: the
index is permanently one hop deeper on that path, and no tool will ever notice.
:::

## Where it bites in production

- **A dangling right link is the corruption you actually see.** Run the lab's per-page logging
  model and crash after step 1: blk 2's `btpo_next` points at a block nothing ever initialized.
  That state is unreachable through a correct WAL implementation, but very reachable through a
  storage stack that reorders or loses writes — a drive that ignores flushes, `nobarrier`, a
  snapshot taken without quiescing. PostgreSQL reports it as
  `index "..." contains unexpected zero page at block N`, with the hint to `REINDEX` it.
- **The symptom before the error is a query that disagrees with itself.** If
  `SELECT count(*) FROM t WHERE k = 40` returns 0 and the same query with
  `SET enable_indexscan = off; SET enable_bitmapscan = off;` returns 1, the index and the heap
  have diverged. That is the first thing to test when a "missing row" ticket arrives.
- **Verify with amcheck, not with a scan.** `bt_index_check()` validates order and sibling links
  under an `AccessShareLock`; `bt_index_parent_check()` additionally verifies that every child
  has a downlink from its parent, at the cost of a `ShareLock` that blocks writes. The parent
  check is the one that speaks to this page's failure mode.
- **Look at the flag directly when you suspect a stuck split.** `pageinspect`'s
  `bt_page_stats()` exposes `btpo_flags` per block; `BTP_INCOMPLETE_SPLIT` showing up on a page
  and *staying* there across repeated inserts means nothing is descending through it to finish
  the job.
- **Splits are a WAL-volume line item.** `pg_waldump --stats=record` breaks WAL down by record
  type, and a random-key insert workload will show `Btree/SPLIT_*` records each carrying the full
  contents of the new right page. The fix is not in the recovery code; it is fill factor and key
  design — the next pages in this module.
- **InnoDB's equivalent is silence.** A crash mid-mini-transaction leaves an incomplete group at
  the end of the redo log, and recovery discards it without comment. There is no flag, no repair
  path and no `amcheck`-shaped tool for it, because the intermediate state never reaches disk in
  the first place.

:::note[What to take forward]
A page split belongs to the index, not to the transaction that caused it, so engines make it
atomic under redo and never undo it — one multi-page WAL record in PostgreSQL, one
mini-transaction in InnoDB, a nested top action with a dummy CLR in the ARIES formulation.
PostgreSQL deliberately leaves the parent downlink for later and records that fact on the page
as `BTP_INCOMPLETE_SPLIT`, so the half-finished tree is a legal state that the next inserter
through the page is obliged to repair. That half-finished state is searchable only because of
the high key and the right link — the same mechanism Lehman-Yao uses for concurrent readers,
which is why the recovery rule and the concurrency rule turn out to be the same rule.
:::
