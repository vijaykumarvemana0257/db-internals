A B+tree's invariants — every leaf at the same depth, every page except the root at least half
full, keys in order — are not maintained by a background process. They are maintained by two
local repairs performed inline by the operation that broke them: a **split** when a page overflows, and a
**merge or redistribution** when one underflows. The split half of that pair is universal.
The merge half is the textbook answer that PostgreSQL declines to implement at all, and which
InnoDB and SQLite each implement differently — which is why a table you deleted 90% of still
has an index the same size as before.

## Insert: one page write, until it isn't

An insert descends from the root comparing against separators, arrives at exactly one leaf,
and writes the entry into that leaf's slot array in key order. That is the whole operation
and it dirties **one page**. No other page in the tree is touched, no ancestor is read again,
nothing rebalances. In a 16 KB InnoDB leaf holding a few hundred rows, or an 8 KB PostgreSQL
leaf holding a few hundred index tuples, this is what nearly every insert does.

The exception is the page that has no room left. Then the engine allocates a new page, moves
roughly half the entries into it, and — this is the part that makes a B+tree a tree rather
than a linked list — teaches the parent about the new page by inserting a **separator key**
and a downlink. Two rules govern that promotion, and they differ by level:

- **Copy-up, from a leaf.** The first key of the new right page becomes the separator, and it
  stays in the leaf as well. It has to: in a B+tree every record lives in a leaf, so a key
  promoted out of one would vanish from the data set. The separator in the parent is a
  *routing* copy.
- **Push-up, from an internal page.** The middle separator moves up and is removed from both
  halves. It is not a record, it is a boundary, and the parent is where that boundary now
  belongs. An internal split of a page holding `cap + 1` separators
  therefore leaves `⌊(cap+1)/2⌋` on the left and the rest on the right, with the promoted key
  living in the parent and in neither half.

Insert enough keys into a full path and the promotion cascades: the leaf splits, the parent
takes a separator and overflows, it splits and pushes up, and so on. If the cascade reaches
the root, the engine allocates a *new* root holding one separator and two downlinks, and the
tree gets one level taller. That is the only way a B+tree grows in height, and it is why all
leaves stay at the same depth — the tree grows from the top, not from the bottom. Drive the
visual's key slider into a full page and watch the cascade; at four keys per page it reaches
the root every few inserts, which at a real fanout of several hundred happens perhaps three
or four times in the life of an index.

Splits are rare per insert and expensive when they happen. Under random keys a page absorbs
roughly `cap/2` inserts between splits, so the amortized cost stays just above one page write
per insert — but the *distribution* is bimodal, and the tail is what your p99 sees.

## What a split actually costs

Count pages, not comparisons. A leaf split in PostgreSQL's nbtree writes:

1. the original page, now holding the left half plus a **high key** equal to the separator
   (the upper bound every nbtree page carries, and the anchor of the right-link protocol);
2. the newly allocated right page, holding the other half;
3. the old right sibling's `btpo_prev` pointer, because leaves are doubly linked and a page
   was just spliced into the chain;
4. the parent, which gains one separator and one downlink.

That is the two-or-three-page split of the textbook plus the parent, and the visual's
"pages written, last op" tile counts exactly these. The WAL cost is the part people
underestimate. nbtree logs steps 1–3 as a **single multi-page record** (`XLOG_BTREE_SPLIT_L`
or `_R`) and the parent's downlink insertion as a *separate* record. If any of those pages is
being written for the first time since the last checkpoint, `full_page_writes` puts an entire
8 KB image of it into the log — so one unlucky split just after a checkpoint can emit 24 KB
of WAL to move a few hundred bytes of keys. You can see this directly: `pg_stat_statements`
reports `wal_records`, `wal_bytes` and `wal_fpi` per statement, and a split-heavy insert
workload is visible as a `wal_fpi` count far above the number of heap pages touched.

The reason those two WAL records are separate — and what happens when a crash lands between
them — is the whole subject of the next page. What matters here is the shape: a split is a
*physical* change to shared structure, several pages at once, and it has to be made
crash-atomic by something other than ordinary transaction rollback.

## Delete: the textbook rules

Removing a key from a leaf is as cheap as inserting one, right up until the page falls below
its occupancy floor of `⌈cap/2⌉` entries. The classical repair has two branches, and the
visual's **textbook** mode runs them:

- **Redistribution.** If an immediate sibling under the same parent has more than the minimum,
  move one entry across and fix the separator in the parent. For leaves the new separator is
  simply the first key of the right page. For internal pages the entry rotates *through* the
  parent: the separator comes down into the underflowing page, and the sibling's outermost
  separator goes up to replace it. Three pages written, tree shape unchanged.
- **Merge.** If no sibling can spare an entry, the two pages fit in one by construction — a
  page with `⌈cap/2⌉ − 1` entries plus a sibling with exactly `⌈cap/2⌉` is at most `cap`. Merging
  leaves simply concatenates them and drops the separator, which described a boundary that no
  longer exists. Merging internal pages must **pull the separator down** between the two key
  runs, because that separator is the only thing that records the boundary between the
  subtrees the merged children point at. Either way the parent loses a key and a downlink, and
  may itself underflow, so the repair can cascade upward exactly as a split cascades.

If the cascade empties the root — an internal root left with a single child and no separators —
the root is freed and its child becomes the new root. Collapsing the root is the only way a
B+tree ever gets *shorter*, and it is the mirror image of the new-root allocation on insert.

:::caution[A `DELETE` statement does not do this]
In PostgreSQL, deleting a row does not touch the index at all: it marks the heap tuple dead
and the matching index entry survives until VACUUM's index cleanup removes it, or until a scan
finds the heap tuple dead and sets `LP_DEAD` so the entry can be killed in place. InnoDB
delete-marks the record and leaves removal to the purge threads. The "delete a key" button in
the visual is the *maintenance* operation, not the SQL one.
:::

## What real engines do instead

| Engine | On underflow | Who reclaims the page | Knob |
|---|---|---|---|
| Textbook B+tree | redistribute, else merge, inline | the operation itself | — |
| PostgreSQL nbtree | **nothing** — a half-empty page stays half empty | VACUUM, and only for pages that are *completely* empty | none; `fillfactor` changes how full pages start out, never how they shrink |
| InnoDB | merge with a sibling if fill drops under `MERGE_THRESHOLD` | the merge itself | `MERGE_THRESHOLD` (default 50), per table or per index |
| SQLite | `balance_nonroot` redistributes cells across the page and up to two siblings | the balance, plus the database freelist | `PRAGMA auto_vacuum`, `VACUUM` |

**PostgreSQL never merges B-tree pages.** There is no code path in nbtree that redistributes
entries between siblings or combines two partially-full pages. The only reclamation is
`_bt_pagedel`, called from VACUUM, and it requires the page to be entirely empty. Deletion then
happens in two logged steps, which is why the intermediate state has a name: first the downlink
is removed from the parent and the page is marked **half-dead** (`BTP_HALF_DEAD`) — unreachable
from above but still in the sibling chain; then the page is unlinked from its left and right
siblings and marked **deleted** (`BTP_DELETED`) with the transaction id that deleted it. A
deleted page cannot be handed out again immediately, because a concurrent scan may still be
holding a pointer to it; it becomes recyclable once that id is old enough, which historically
meant waiting for a *later* VACUUM. PostgreSQL 14 added a pending-FSM pass so that pages
deleted during a VACUUM can usually be recycled by that same VACUUM. One more rule the visual
enforces: nbtree will not delete the rightmost page of a level, so the right edge of the tree
is never reclaimed.

Switch the visual to **PostgreSQL nbtree** mode, delete a dozen keys, and watch what does not
happen: fill bars go orange, height stays where it is, and the page count never drops. Press
VACUUM and the empty pages march one stage per press — empty, half-dead, deleted, free — and
only then does the next split reuse one instead of extending the file.

**InnoDB merges, but lazily and by bytes.** When a delete (or an update that shortens a
record) drops a page's fill below `MERGE_THRESHOLD` percent of the page, InnoDB attempts to
merge it with a neighbouring page under the same parent; if the pages do not fit together, the
attempt simply fails and the page stays under-full. The threshold defaults to 50 and is set
with a table or index comment — `CREATE TABLE … COMMENT='MERGE_THRESHOLD=40'`, or the same
comment on an individual index. Both halves of the outcome are counted:
`INFORMATION_SCHEMA.INNODB_METRICS` exposes `index_page_merge_attempts` and
`index_page_merge_successful`, alongside `index_page_splits`. A large gap between attempts and
successes is the signature of a workload thrashing around the threshold, and lowering
`MERGE_THRESHOLD` is the documented response. The visual's threshold slider and its
`merges / attempts` tile model exactly this pair.

**SQLite rebalances across three pages, not two.** Its `balance_nonroot` takes the affected
page plus up to two siblings and redistributes all their cells evenly, emitting one more or one
fewer page than it consumed as needed — so it covers the split case and the merge case with one
routine, and it can leave three pages where the textbook would have left two half-empty ones.
Deletes trigger it once a page is mostly free space. The root is a special case for a reason
peculiar to SQLite: a table's root page number is recorded in `sqlite_schema.rootpage`, so the
root page cannot be *replaced*. `balance_deeper` instead copies the root's contents into a
freshly allocated child and leaves the root page number intact. Freed pages go on the database
freelist for reuse; the file itself only shrinks under `VACUUM` or incremental auto-vacuum.

## Why refusing to merge is defensible

Eager merging looks like the obviously correct policy and mostly isn't.

- **It thrashes.** A workload oscillating around the half-full boundary — delete one key, merge,
  insert one key, split — pays several page writes and a WAL record per operation for a tree
  whose shape never actually changes. The engines that merge lazily (InnoDB's threshold,
  SQLite's two-thirds-free test) are buying hysteresis.
- **The space usually comes back.** An index page emptied by a range of deletes is normally
  refilled by subsequent inserts in the same key range. Merging it now costs writes to reclaim
  space you are about to need.
- **It is the hardest operation to make concurrent.** A merge removes a page that another
  descending reader may already be pointed at, which is precisely the case a simple latching
  scheme cannot handle — and it is why a merge in nbtree would have to interact with the
  right-link protocol that makes concurrent descents safe. That interaction is the subject of
  the later concurrency module; the relevant fact here is that avoiding merges entirely made
  PostgreSQL's concurrent B-tree simpler, and that was a deliberate trade.

The cost of the trade is bloat, and it is real: an index whose keys were deleted in a pattern
that leaves every page 20% full occupies five times the space it needs, keeps five times as
many pages in the buffer pool, and makes a range scan read five times as many pages.

## Where it bites in production

- **The bulk delete that frees nothing.** `DELETE FROM events WHERE ts < now() - interval '90 days'`
  removes most entries from the low end of an index and PostgreSQL returns the space only for
  pages that end up completely empty — and then only to *that index*, for future inserts. The
  file never shrinks. `pgstatindex()` from the `pgstattuple` extension is the measurement:
  `avg_leaf_density` well under the ~90% a fresh index shows, plus a large `deleted_pages` or
  `empty_pages` count. The fix is `REINDEX INDEX CONCURRENTLY` (PostgreSQL 12 and later), not
  more VACUUM.
- **Partition dropping beats deleting.** The reason range partitioning is the standard answer to
  retention is exactly this page: `DROP TABLE` on a partition reclaims the index in full, while
  a `DELETE` of the same rows leaves a half-empty tree and a VACUUM bill.
- **Split storms after a checkpoint.** A monotonic insert workload concentrates splits on the
  rightmost page; each split immediately after a checkpoint writes full-page images of every
  page it touches. The symptom is periodic WAL-volume spikes correlated with checkpoint
  boundaries, visible in `pg_stat_statements`' `wal_fpi` and against the checkpoint counts in
  `pg_stat_bgwriter` (`pg_stat_checkpointer` from PostgreSQL 17).
- **InnoDB counters that disagree.** `index_page_merge_attempts` climbing far faster than
  `index_page_merge_successful`, or `index_page_splits` high on a table that is not growing,
  says the tree is churning; a lower `MERGE_THRESHOLD` or an `OPTIMIZE TABLE` rebuild is the
  lever.
- **Verifying the invariants.** When you suspect the tree itself rather than its occupancy,
  PostgreSQL's `amcheck` extension has `bt_index_check()` — and `bt_index_parent_check()` for
  the stricter parent/child downlink verification — which check exactly the invariants this
  page maintains: ordering, downlink correctness, and the sibling chain.

:::note[What to take forward]
Splits are universal and cheap-until-they-aren't: one page write per insert, then two or three
pages plus a parent and a multi-page WAL record when a page overflows, with the tree growing
only at the root. Merges are the half of the algorithm real engines negotiate away — PostgreSQL
declines entirely and reclaims only wholly empty pages through VACUUM's half-dead/deleted
protocol, InnoDB merges under `MERGE_THRESHOLD`, SQLite rebalances across three siblings. Plan
for indexes that do not shrink when rows are deleted, and reach for partition drops or
`REINDEX CONCURRENTLY` rather than expecting the tree to tidy itself.
:::
