A B+tree's height is decided by its fanout and barely moves. What moves — by a factor of two
in size, and by orders of magnitude in range-scan cost — is how full its leaves are and
whether the sibling chain still runs forward through the file. Both are decided by the shape
of the key stream you feed it, and the only knob you get is a number that applies to one of
the two kinds of split.

## What fillfactor actually reserves

`fillfactor` is a per-relation storage parameter that says how full to pack a page while
leaving room for what comes later. For a PostgreSQL B-tree the default is **90** for leaf
pages (`BTREE_DEFAULT_FILLFACTOR`); internal pages use a fixed 70
(`BTREE_NONLEAF_FILLFACTOR`) and are not settable. You set it per index:

```sql
CREATE INDEX orders_placed_at_idx ON orders (placed_at) WITH (fillfactor = 70);
ALTER INDEX orders_placed_at_idx SET (fillfactor = 70);   -- future splits use it; existing pages change only at REINDEX
```

InnoDB's equivalent is `innodb_fill_factor`, a server variable defaulting to 100 that governs
sorted index builds. Even at 100 InnoDB does not fill a clustered-index page to the brim: it
leaves **1/16 of the page** free for future growth, which is where the "15/16" number in
InnoDB write-ups comes from. On the other side, InnoDB will merge a page into a sibling when
its fill drops below `MERGE_THRESHOLD`, which defaults to 50% and is settable per index in a
table comment.

The reason to reserve room is not the same for a heap and for an index. In a heap, free space
on the page is what lets an `UPDATE` be a **HOT update** — a new tuple version on the same
page, with no index entry to write — which is why an update-heavy table is often given
`fillfactor = 85`. In an index, reserved space absorbs the *next* key that sorts into that
page without forcing a split. That is the whole benefit, and it is only available if new keys
actually sort into pages that have room.

:::caution[The fillfactor misconception]
Setting `fillfactor` on a B-tree index does **not** cause pages to be maintained at that
density. PostgreSQL applies the leaf fillfactor in exactly two places: when a page is built
from sorted input (`CREATE INDEX`, `REINDEX`), and when the **rightmost** page of a level
splits. Every other split is at the midpoint, 50/50. So on a randomly-inserted index,
`fillfactor = 70` changes nothing at all after the rebuild has decayed — the pages fill to
100%, split in half, and converge right back to where they were. Toggle the key stream in the
lab from bigserial to UUIDv4 and drag the fillfactor slider: the density stat barely moves off
ln 2 whatever you set, while the same drag on bigserial moves it point for point.
:::

## Two split points, and why the insert position picks one

When a leaf overflows, the engine must decide where to cut it. `nbtsplitloc.c`'s
`_bt_findsplitloc` evaluates candidate split points and applies the leaf fillfactor when the
page is the rightmost one on its level: the left half keeps `fillfactor` percent of the space
and everything after it moves to the new right page. For a strictly ascending key stream that
is the *only* kind of split that ever happens, so the pattern is: fill a page to capacity,
split, leave 90% behind, carry ~10% plus the new tuple forward, repeat. Steady-state leaf
density lands at roughly the fillfactor, and the file grows strictly left to right.

PostgreSQL 12 moved split-point selection out of `nbtinsert.c` into `nbtsplitloc.c`, where a
set of secondary heuristics live alongside the fillfactor rule: a "split after the new item"
case for when the incoming tuple sits at the end of the page, and a distinct strategy for
pages full of duplicates. The naive midpoint rule wastes half of every page an append-only
workload creates, and these are the rules that stop it.

InnoDB reaches the same conclusion through the page header rather than through the tree
shape. Each index page records `PAGE_LAST_INSERT`, `PAGE_DIRECTION` and `PAGE_N_DIRECTION`;
after a run of inserts in the same direction, `btr_page_split_and_insert` calls
`btr_page_get_split_rec_to_right` (or `..._to_left`) and splits **at the insertion point**
instead of at the midpoint, so the page being appended to stays packed and the new page starts
nearly empty.

Uncheck **Rightmost-page split optimization** in the lab with bigserial selected. Every split
becomes a midpoint split, each page freezes at half full the moment the next one is created,
and the same rows need close to twice the pages — the textbook algorithm producing a 50%-dense
index on the most common key pattern in OLTP.

There is a second optimization for ascending keys, on the descent rather than the split.
PostgreSQL 11 added a **fastpath insert**: `_bt_doinsert` caches the block number of the
rightmost leaf on the relation, and if the incoming key sorts after that page's high key it
conditionally locks the cached buffer and inserts there directly, skipping the root-to-leaf
descent entirely. A `bigserial` primary key therefore costs one buffer access per insert
instead of three or four.

## Random keys: the 69% ceiling

Now feed the same tree UUIDv4. Each key is 122 random bits, so each insert lands on a
uniformly random leaf, and every one of those leaves is an interior page of the level. The
fillfactor rule never fires; every split is a midpoint split.

The steady state of that process is a classical result: under random insertions, B-tree node
occupancy converges to **ln 2 ≈ 69.3%** (Yao, 1978). Pages oscillate between 50% right after a
split and 100% just before the next one, and the time-average is ln 2, not 75%, because a page
that just split takes longer to refill than a nearly-full page takes to overflow. That is the
"50–70% fill" you see quoted for randomly-keyed indexes, and it is a floor you cannot tune
away — it is a property of the split rule, not of a setting.

The density is the least of it. Three other costs arrive with random keys:

- **The write working set becomes the whole index.** Watch the "leaves touched, last 200
  inserts" stat. Ascending keys touch only the handful of pages the right
  edge advanced through; UUIDv4 touches very nearly every leaf in the index, each of which must
  be read into the buffer pool, dirtied, and eventually written. Once the index
  exceeds memory, every insert becomes a random read *and* a random write.
- **Full-page writes multiply.** The first time a page is dirtied after a checkpoint,
  PostgreSQL writes the entire 8 KB image into the WAL (`full_page_writes`, on by default, and
  the reason `wal_compression` exists). Scattering inserts across thousands of pages per
  checkpoint interval means thousands of full-page images instead of a handful.
- **The key is twice as wide.** A `bigint` index entry costs 20 bytes on the page (8-byte
  `IndexTupleData` header + 8-byte key + 4-byte line pointer); a `uuid` costs 28. On an 8 KB
  leaf with 8152 usable bytes that is about 400 entries versus about 290 — before the density
  difference. Store the same UUID as `char(36)` text and the tuple MAXALIGNs to 48 bytes,
  52 with its line pointer, so about 155 per page — the single most expensive schema mistake
  in this area.

In InnoDB the damage is larger still, because the clustered index *is* the table: a random
primary key scatters the **rows**, not just index entries, and every secondary index carries a
copy of that 16-byte PK as its row pointer.

## Fragmentation: when key order stops being file order

A split needs a new page. A growing index has no free pages to hand back, so the new block is
allocated at the end of the relation file and the sibling pointer of the page being split is
redirected to it. Key order and block order are identical only until the first non-rightmost
split; after that, walking the leaf chain means walking backwards and forwards through the
file.

That is exactly what the lines in the lab encode. Top row is the sibling chain in key order;
bottom row is the same pages in block-number order — the order a sequential read returns them.
Under bigserial the lines are parallel. Under UUIDv4 they are a mesh, and every crossing is a
seek some range scan will pay.

PostgreSQL will tell you the numbers directly:

```sql
CREATE EXTENSION IF NOT EXISTS pgstattuple;
SELECT * FROM pgstatindex('orders_id_idx');
--  version | tree_level | index_size | root_block_no | internal_pages | leaf_pages
--  | empty_pages | deleted_pages | avg_leaf_density | leaf_fragmentation
```

`avg_leaf_density` is the occupancy number above — around 90 on a freshly built index, drifting
toward 69 under random inserts, and far lower on an index whose keys are deleted in bulk.
`leaf_fragmentation` is deliberately conservative: it counts the percentage of leaf pages whose
right sibling sits at a **lower** block number than the page itself. A forward jump of 500
blocks does not count. So a non-zero `leaf_fragmentation` is not a warning, it is proof the
chain is genuinely scrambled — and the true random-I/O cost is always worse than the number
suggests.

The stat that matters for a query is the one the lab calls **runs**: the number of maximal
stretches of consecutive blocks in the part of the chain a scan touches. One run means the
kernel's readahead and the engine's prefetching see a sequential stream and can fetch a
megabyte at a time. Forty runs over forty leaves means forty independent I/Os at queue depth
one — on NVMe that trades one large sequential transfer for forty serialised round-trips, and
on network-attached storage or spinning disk the gap is orders of magnitude, not a factor.
Widen the range-scan slider and watch runs grow linearly with the window on
a fragmented index and stay at 1 on a clean one.

:::note[This is not the same as heap correlation]
`pg_stats.correlation` measures whether the *heap* is in index order, and drives the planner's
choice between an index scan and a bitmap heap scan. `leaf_fragmentation` measures whether the
*index's own leaf pages* are in key order. A table can be perfectly clustered while its index is
shredded, and vice versa. The planner models the first and not the second, which is one reason a
fragmented index produces plans that look fine and run slowly.
:::

## The rightmost leaf is one page, and everyone wants it

The reward for ascending keys is a dense index in file order. The bill is that every concurrent
inserter wants the same buffer. Watch the "inserts on the rightmost leaf" stat sit at 100% for
bigserial: with 64 connections inserting, that is 64 sessions serialising on one page's content
lock, plus the fastpath's conditional lock attempt, plus — when the page finally splits — a
brief exclusive hold while two pages and a parent are updated.

This shows up by name in every engine. SQL Server calls it *last-page insert contention* and
you see it as `PAGELATCH_EX` waits on one page of one index; SQL Server 2019 added the index
option `OPTIMIZE_FOR_SEQUENTIAL_KEY` specifically to schedule those waiters instead of letting
them convoy. Oracle's traditional answer is a **reverse key index**, which byte-reverses the key
so consecutive values land on different blocks — and thereby destroys range scans on that index
completely — or a hash-partitioned index, which spreads the hot spot across N right edges while
keeping local ordering. InnoDB users hit it as index-page latch contention on the clustered
index's right edge.

The protocols that make concurrent descents safe while a split is in flight — latch crabbing,
Lehman–Yao high keys, Blink trees — are the subject of their own module. What belongs here is
the shape of the trade: **fully ordered keys maximise density and locality and minimise the
number of pages under contention to exactly one.** Fully random keys do the reverse on every
axis. You are picking a point on that line.

## UUIDv7, ULID, and getting both

The point that gets you most of both is a time-ordered identifier. RFC 9562 (2024) standardised
**UUIDv7**: a 48-bit big-endian Unix millisecond timestamp followed by random bits, so values
generated in different milliseconds sort in generation order while values inside one millisecond
are unordered. ULID has the same layout (48-bit ms + 80 random bits) in a different textual
encoding. Select UUIDv7 in the lab: density returns to the fillfactor, fragmentation goes to
zero, the write working set collapses to a handful of leaves — and the rightmost-leaf share drops
below 100%, because the intra-millisecond scramble spreads inserts over the last page or two,
which is a mild contention win as well.

Practical notes that matter more than the encoding:

- **PostgreSQL 18** ships a built-in `uuidv7()` (and `uuidv4()`); before that, generate it in
  the application or use an extension. Store it in the `uuid` type, never text.
- **MySQL** has had `UUID_TO_BIN(uuid, swap_flag)` since 8.0: with `swap_flag = 1` it reorders a
  UUIDv1's time fields into big-endian order and returns 16 bytes, turning a scattering PK into
  a monotonic one. `BINARY(16)` with a swapped UUID is the standard InnoDB pattern.
- **Third option: don't use a UUID as the clustered key at all.** A `bigserial`/`AUTO_INCREMENT`
  primary key with a separate unique UUID column gives the dense clustered index and keeps the
  externally-visible opaque identifier; the cost is one extra random-keyed unique index instead
  of all of them.
- **UUIDv7 leaks creation time** to anyone holding an ID. If that matters, it is a real reason
  to accept v4 and pay for it in fill.

This is also the one case where fillfactor is doing exactly the job it was designed for. Drag
the slider to 100 with UUIDv7 selected and watch fragmentation jump: packing the left page full
means the next key that jitters backwards into it — an ordinary event inside one millisecond —
splits a page that is no longer the rightmost one, at the midpoint, with a new block at the end
of the file. The reserved 10% is what absorbs that jitter.

The lab's fourth stream — `(created_at, id)` at second resolution — is the common composite
case, and it separates two things that are easy to conflate. Locality stays excellent: the write
working set is a handful of leaves and the range-scan window stays inside a narrow region of the
file. But the hot band is several pages wide, so splits inside it are midpoint splits: density
falls back toward 70% and block order scrambles *locally*. Coarser timestamps widen the band and
give more scatter, not less.

## Repair, and where it bites

Nothing reclaims index fill or reorders blocks incrementally. PostgreSQL never merges
half-empty B-tree pages back together; emptied pages are eventually recycled into the same
index's free space map, so the file does not shrink and the chain does not straighten. The
repairs are all rebuilds:

| Action | What it does | Locking |
|---|---|---|
| `REINDEX INDEX x` | Rebuilds from a fresh sort, packed to `fillfactor` | `SHARE` on the parent table (reads fine, writes blocked) plus `ACCESS EXCLUSIVE` on the index |
| `REINDEX INDEX CONCURRENTLY x` | Same, building a shadow index and swapping it (PostgreSQL 12+) | No blocking of reads/writes; two passes, and it leaves an `_ccnew` invalid index behind if it fails |
| `pg_repack` | Rebuilds table and indexes via triggers and a swap | Brief exclusive lock at the swap |
| `OPTIMIZE TABLE t` (InnoDB) | Mapped to `ALTER TABLE ... FORCE`: an online rebuild of the clustered index and all secondaries, with the note *"Table does not support optimize, doing recreate + analyze instead"* | Online (`ALGORITHM=INPLACE`) unless `--skip-new` / `old_alter_table` |

Rebuilding also needs the space: `REINDEX CONCURRENTLY` holds the old and new index at once, so
plan for the index's size in free disk, and remember that the rebuild is a full write of the
index into the WAL.

What this looks like in production:

- **An index bigger than its table.** A UUIDv4 primary key on a narrow row, at 69% fill with
  28-byte entries, routinely produces an index in the same order of magnitude as the heap.
  `\di+` plus `pg_relation_size` against row counts is the check; `pgstatindex` tells you whether
  it is density, bloat from deletes, or both.
- **A range scan whose plan did not change but whose runtime tripled.** Same `Index Scan`, same
  row estimate, more I/O — `EXPLAIN (ANALYZE, BUFFERS)` shows the shared block reads climbing
  while the rows returned stay flat. That is fragmentation, and `pgstatindex` confirms it.
- **p99 insert latency on one statement while CPU is idle.** Lock/latch waits on a single index
  page: `pg_stat_activity.wait_event` showing `BufferContent` on a hot index, or
  `PAGELATCH_EX` in SQL Server, both pointing at the rightmost leaf.
- **Checkpoint-time write storms** proportional to the scatter of your keys, because every
  distinct page dirtied since the last checkpoint has to be written and the first touch of each
  emitted a full-page image into the WAL.
- **A nightly REINDEX in cron.** It works, which is why it survives; it is also a signal that
  the key choice is wrong, because a time-ordered key needs no maintenance at all.

:::note[What to take forward]
Fillfactor is a *build-time and rightmost-split* knob, not a maintained density — on a randomly
keyed index it does nothing once the rebuild has decayed to ln 2 ≈ 69%. Ascending keys buy
density, file order and a one-page working set, and pay for them with contention on a single
leaf; random keys pay everywhere and buy nothing but opacity. Time-ordered identifiers —
UUIDv7, ULID, a swapped `UUID_TO_BIN` — are how you get the first set without the second, and
they are the only fix that does not need a recurring rebuild.
:::
