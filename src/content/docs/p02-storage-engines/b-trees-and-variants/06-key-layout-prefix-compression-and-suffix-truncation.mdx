Fanout is not a property of a B+tree. It is a property of the bytes you put in one, and the
engine is allowed to be creative about which bytes those are. An internal page does not need
to store your keys — it needs to store just enough of them to decide which child to descend
into — and a leaf page does not need to store the same 40-byte tenant name eight hundred
times. Every byte removed from a cell multiplies the number of children a page can route,
which shrinks the internal levels until they fit in memory and stay there.

## A cell is not a key

An index page is a slotted page, laid out exactly as
[Page anatomy and the slotted page](../../../p02-storage-engines/pages-tuples-heap-files/01-page-anatomy-and-the-slotted-page/)
describes: a fixed header at the low end, an array of fixed-width line pointers growing up
from it, and variable-length cells growing down from the high end. What changes for an index
is what goes in the cell and what the slot array means.

In PostgreSQL's nbtree the page has a 24-byte `PageHeaderData`, a 16-byte `BTPageOpaqueData`
special area at the very end (left and right sibling links, level, flags), and between them
4-byte `ItemIdData` line pointers and the tuples themselves. A tuple is an 8-byte
`IndexTupleData` header — a 6-byte `t_tid` plus a 2-byte `t_info` — followed by the key
data, `MAXALIGN`ed to 8. The row pointer is not extra: in a leaf tuple `t_tid` *is* the heap
TID. So a leaf entry for a 36-character UUID stored as `text` costs
`MAXALIGN(8 + 1 + 36) + 4` = 52 bytes, not 36 — the 1 is the short varlena length byte, the
other 15 are alignment padding, the tuple header and the line pointer.

Two details matter later. First, unlike the heap, **the line pointer array is in key order** —
nbtree keeps items sorted by offset number precisely so that an intra-page lookup is a binary
search over a contiguous 4-byte array rather than a walk. Second, in an internal page the
downlink is not stored separately: the child block number lives in `t_tid`, the same six
bytes that hold a heap TID in a leaf. An internal cell therefore costs
`MAXALIGN(8 + separator bytes) + 4` and nothing more — which makes the separator's width the
single number that sets fanout.

## Separators only have to separate

The B+tree invariant for an internal entry is weak on purpose: a separator `S` between two
children must satisfy `last key of the left child ≤ S ≤ first key of the right child` (the
exact inequality depends on whether the engine treats the separator as inclusive). Nothing
requires `S` to be a key that exists, or to be a complete key at all. Anything that lands in
the gap routes correctly.

PostgreSQL 12 turned that into **suffix truncation**. When a leaf splits, `_bt_truncate`
compares the last key on the left page with the first key on the right, finds the first
attribute where they differ, keeps attributes up to and including that one, and throws the
rest away; the count of surviving attributes is stamped into the tuple header, so the
resulting *pivot tuple* is a legal, self-describing, shorter key. On an index over
`(tenant, event_type, occurred_at, id)` where the boundary falls inside a tenant's event
stream, the separator keeps `tenant`, `event_type` and `occurred_at` and drops a 36-byte
UUID and the 6-byte heap TID — a third of the cell, gone, on every internal page.

Three consequences are worth internalising:

- **Non-key `INCLUDE` columns are always truncated away.** A covering index's payload exists
  only in leaves, so adding `INCLUDE (amount, status)` widens leaf cells and leaves internal
  fanout untouched.
- **The split point is chosen to make truncation pay.** `_bt_findsplitloc` evaluates many
  candidate split points rather than just the midpoint, and among those that satisfy the
  fillfactor it prefers the one that yields the smallest separator. A split that lands on a
  tenant boundary produces a one-attribute separator; the engine will go looking for it.
- **Heap TID is the implicit last column.** Since PostgreSQL 12 the heap TID participates in
  the key ordering, which gives every entry a unique position — and truncation removes it
  from separators. When the two boundary keys are equal in every real column, truncation has
  nothing to drop and the separator keeps the whole key plus a TID; toggle the low-cardinality
  key shape in the lab and watch the separator stop shrinking for exactly that reason.

Not every engine does this. InnoDB internal records carry the complete key, and because a
secondary index's key *is* `(indexed columns, primary key columns)`, that includes the whole
PK. SQLite's index interior cells carry the full index record. WiredTiger does truncate, at
byte rather than attribute granularity, under the table config `internal_key_truncate`.

:::note[Fanout buys residency more often than it buys height]
Height is `ceil(log_F N)` and F sits inside a logarithm, so raising fanout from 60 to 90
rarely removes a level. What it removes is *internal pages*: at fanout F the internal levels
are about `1/(F−1)` of the index, so a 1.5 GB index has ~25 MB of internal pages at F=60 and
~17 MB at F=90. That is the difference between the routing structure living permanently in
the buffer pool and competing with leaves for frames.
:::

## The high key on every page

Every nbtree page except the rightmost of its level stores, at offset 1, a **high key**: an
upper bound on every key the page may contain. It is the same truncated pivot tuple that was
copied up to the parent at split time, kept in two places on purpose. A descent that arrives
at a page and finds its search key greater than the high key knows it is at a stale page and
must follow `btpo_next` to the right — the Lehman-Yao rule that lets readers proceed without
holding a lock on the parent, and the reason an
[incomplete split](../../../p02-storage-engines/b-trees-and-variants/03-crash-safety-of-structure-modifications-atomic-splits-and-in/)
is still searchable. (The locking protocol built on it belongs to the concurrency module.)

For this page, the high key is a cost: one extra cell per page, which is why the lab subtracts
a separator-sized tuple from every page's usable space before packing it. It is also the
clearest argument for truncation — the narrower the separator, the cheaper it is to carry one
on every page in the index.

## Prefix compression: pay once for the shared bytes

Sorted keys share leading bytes with their neighbours almost by definition. **Front coding**
(the classic name; "prefix compression" in storage engines) stores, per cell, the number of
bytes shared with the preceding cell plus only the bytes that differ. On a page of
`acme-logistics-eu-west-1|checkout.session.completed|2026-03-14T…|<uuid>` keys, the first cell
stores all 110 bytes; its neighbours share the tenant, the event type and most of the
timestamp, so they store barely half that.

The cost is that a cell is no longer independently readable. A binary search needs to compare
against an arbitrary item, and an item that begins "…share 47 bytes with whatever came before
me" cannot be compared without reconstructing it. Engines resolve this in one of two ways:

- **Restart points.** Every *k*-th cell stores its key in full and front coding restarts
  there; the binary search runs over restart points and the final few comparisons are a linear
  walk. RocksDB does exactly this inside data blocks, with `block_restart_interval` defaulting
  to 16.
- **Instantiation.** WiredTiger keeps pages prefix-compressed in its on-disk image and
  materialises individual keys in memory on demand by walking back to a preceding complete
  key, gated by `prefix_compression` and a `prefix_compression_min` threshold so it does not
  spend a length byte to save three.

MyISAM's `PACK_KEYS` table option is the same idea, shipped decades earlier — packed
(front-coded) index blocks, cheap to store, more expensive to search. PostgreSQL's nbtree has never shipped
prefix compression; its page-at-a-time binary search assumes every item is directly
comparable, and the community has repeatedly judged the reconstruction cost and the
complication of every page-modification path to outweigh the space.

Notice in the lab what the technique does *not* do: switch to the UUIDv4 key shape and prefix
compression saves nothing at all, because adjacent random keys share nothing. Prefix
compression is a bet on correlated keys — timestamps, paths, tenant-scoped identifiers, sorted
text — and against random ones, and it is the reason a synthetic benchmark over random strings
can conclude it is worthless.

## Deduplication: one key, many TIDs

PostgreSQL 13 added the other half of the leaf-side story. A secondary index on
`(status)` over ten million rows stores the string `'delivered'` a few million times, each in
its own 24-byte tuple, purely so that each can carry a different 6-byte TID. **Deduplication**
folds a run of equal keys into a single *posting list tuple*: the key once, followed by a
sorted array of `ItemPointerData`s. The per-duplicate cost falls from a whole aligned tuple
plus a line pointer to six bytes.

The mechanics are worth knowing because they explain when it does and does not fire:

- It is **lazy**. A deduplication pass runs when a leaf page is about to split — the engine
  would rather compact than split — not on every insert. So the physical layout of a freshly
  built index and one that has been written into for a month differ.
- It is bounded by the ordinary item limit. A single index tuple cannot exceed roughly a third
  of a page (PostgreSQL will tell you so with `index row size 3096 exceeds btree version 4
  maximum 2704 for index "…"`), and a posting list is an index tuple, so a long duplicate run
  becomes several posting tuples of a few hundred TIDs each rather than one giant one.
- It requires **`equalimage`**. The opclass must provide a support function asserting that
  equal values have identical binary images. `numeric` does not qualify — `1.0` and `1.00` are
  equal and store differently — nor does `float`, nor `text` under a nondeterministic
  collation. Those indexes silently get no deduplication. The per-index escape hatch is the
  storage parameter `deduplicate_items = off`.
- It applies to **unique indexes too**, where the duplicates are not user data but dead row
  versions from repeated updates. Together with bottom-up index deletion in PostgreSQL 14, it
  is why a table updated in place no longer grows its indexes without bound.

Drag the lab to the low-cardinality key shape and turn deduplication on: leaf pages stop being
mostly tuple headers, and the index collapses by roughly the duplicate-run factor. Turn on the
UUID shape and the toggle does nothing, because there is nothing to fold.

## Wide keys are a tax on every level

InnoDB's secondary indexes do not store a row pointer; they store the **primary key** of the
row, because the table *is* the clustered B+tree and the PK is how you get back to it. That
makes the PK width a multiplier on every other index. A `BIGINT` PK adds 8 bytes to every
secondary entry; a `CHAR(36)` UUID adds 36 in a single-byte charset (and counts as 144 against
the key-length limit under `utf8mb4`, which reserves the maximum bytes per character), and because the PK columns are part of the
secondary key, they also appear in every internal record — InnoDB does no suffix truncation,
so the wide PK shrinks fanout at every level, not just at the leaves. Choose the
InnoDB-style key shape in the lab and turn suffix truncation *off* — InnoDB's behaviour — to
see both effects at once.

That is the concrete cost behind the "use a narrow, monotonic primary key" advice, and it
compounds with the split behaviour of random keys covered in
[Fill factor, fragmentation and splits under load](../../../p02-storage-engines/b-trees-and-variants/04-fill-factor-fragmentation-and-splits-under-load/).
InnoDB also enforces a hard ceiling: 3072 bytes of index key with the `DYNAMIC` or
`COMPRESSED` row format at the default 16 KB page, 767 with `COMPACT` or `REDUNDANT`, which is
why long `VARCHAR` columns end up indexed by a prefix (`KEY (url(191))`).

## Normalized keys, and the bridge to the next page

Every technique on this page is easier when a key is a **byte string that compares correctly
under `memcmp`**. Truncation at byte granularity, front coding, and the SIMD-friendly
comparison loops inside a page all become trivial byte operations, and impossible or
approximate otherwise — nbtree truncates at *attribute* granularity precisely because its keys
are typed datums compared by per-type functions, not bytes.

Systems that control their own encoding go the other way and normalize first. MyRocks, TiKV,
CockroachDB and FoundationDB's tuple layer all encode composite keys into a single
memcomparable byte string: integers sign-flipped and stored big-endian, NULLs given a reserved
leading tag byte, embedded zero bytes escaped so no key is a prefix of another, descending
columns bitwise complemented. PostgreSQL uses a partial version of the same idea in sorting —
`SortSupport` abbreviated keys pack a lossy 8-byte normalized prefix into the sort tuple so
most comparisons are a single integer compare, with the full comparator as a tiebreak.

That encoding is also where the ordering guarantees of the whole tree come from, and where
they can be silently lost. The next page takes it apart:
[Collation, Unicode and byte-comparable key encoding](../../../p02-storage-engines/b-trees-and-variants/07-collation-unicode-and-byte-comparable-key-encoding/).

## Where it bites in production

- **The index is bigger than the table.** Usually a wide composite key, a `text` PK, or
  duplicates that cannot deduplicate. Check `pg_relation_size` per index, then
  `pgstatindex(...)` from `pgstattuple` for `avg_leaf_density` and `leaf_fragmentation`, and
  `index_size / (tuples × key width)` for how much of it is overhead.
- **A major-version upgrade changes index sizes.** Rebuilding indexes after moving to
  PostgreSQL 12 or 13 (via `REINDEX`, or `pg_upgrade` plus a reindex) is what actually
  materialises suffix truncation and deduplication — the on-disk pages you carried over keep
  their old layout. An index that shrinks by half on `REINDEX` is not a bug report.
- **Deduplication that never fires.** An index on a `numeric` column, or on `text` in a
  nondeterministic (`ICU`, case-insensitive) collation, gets none of it and nothing says so.
  If a low-cardinality index is unexpectedly large, check the column's type and collation
  before blaming vacuum.
- **`Specified key was too long; max key length is 3072 bytes`** on MySQL, or
  `index row size … exceeds btree version 4 maximum 2704` on PostgreSQL: the key you are
  indexing is not an identifier, it is a document. Index a hash, a prefix, or an expression.
- **Prefix compression on random keys** costs CPU on every page search and saves nothing.
  It is a per-table decision in WiredTiger for a reason.

:::note[What to take forward]
An index's real currency is bytes per cell, and each level of the tree can spend them
differently: leaves must hold the whole key plus a row pointer, internal pages only need
enough bytes to route. Suffix truncation exploits that asymmetry to raise fanout and keep the
routing levels resident; prefix compression and deduplication attack the leaves instead, and
both are bets on the shape of your data — correlated neighbours, or repeated values. A wide
clustered primary key is the opposite of all three: it inflates every cell of every secondary
index, at every level.
:::
