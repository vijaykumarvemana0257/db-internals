A B-tree page in the buffer pool is shared memory. Every descent reads the root, and at any moment one thread may
be splitting a leaf: moving half its keys to a new page and adding a separator to the parent. A reader that followed
a downlink just before that split and reaches the page just after it arrives at a page that no longer holds its key.
Every protocol here answers one question: **how does a descent know that the page it reached still covers its key?**

These are **latches**, not locks: short-term mutual exclusion on an in-memory page, held for one page visit or one
structural change, never for a transaction. No deadlock detector ever sees them, so each protocol avoids deadlock by
construction: by the order in which it takes them, or by never waiting at all.

The lab's tree has three levels and at most three keys per page. The inserter adds **27** to the full leaf
L2 `[20, 25, 30]`, so L2 splits and 27 and 30 end up on a new page, L2′. The reader looks up **30**, the key that moves. Separators
are inclusive upper bounds, the convention PostgreSQL's nbtree uses, so each page's high key equals its parent's
separator for it. **Race to the end** lets the reader descend until it is about to enter the leaf, then runs the
inserter while it can; a thread that would wait makes one attempt, so the wait is recorded, and yields.

## Latch crabbing

The oldest protocol, from Bayer and Schkolnick (1977), is called latch coupling, hand-over-hand locking or
**crabbing**. The rule: **latch the child before releasing the parent**, so a split can never slip into a gap.
Readers take shared (S) latches and hold at most two. Writers take exclusive (X) latches and keep every ancestor until
they latch a **safe** node — one the operation cannot change beyond itself:

- **for an insert**, a node that is not full, so a split cannot climb above it;
- **for a delete**, a node more than half full, so a merge cannot climb above it.

On latching a safe child, the writer releases all ancestors; from the lowest safe node down, everything stays
X-latched until the change is done.

Select **Latch crabbing** and press **Race to the end**. The reader holds S on P until it has latched L2, so the
inserter, already holding X on the root, waits for P; then it sees P has room, releases R, and waits again for L2
while the reader finds 30 there. The inserter held three pages at once: P, L2 and the new L2′. Tick **Parent P is
full too**: P is no longer safe, R stays latched, and **Most pages the inserter held at once** reaches 5. Untick it,
reset, step the inserter three times and the reader twice: the reader's S request on P now waits for the whole split,
and **Race to the end** then sends it straight to L2′.

The flaw is the first step: **every insert X-latches the root**. Choose **12 — into L1 (has room)**. The inserter
still takes X on R, still waits for the reader at P, and briefly holds R and P together. On many cores that one root
latch serializes every writer of the index.

### Optimistic descent

The usual fix is to bet that the leaf will not split: descend with S latches like a reader, X-latch only the leaf,
and if the leaf is unsafe, release it and start again pessimistically. Tick **Optimistic descent first**. With 12,
the inserter never waits. With 27, it reaches L2, finds it full and restarts once. The bet pays because a page that
has just split is half empty. Graefe's survey of B-tree locking notes the weak spot: with variable-length keys "safe"
is not a count, because room depends on the size of the incoming key and of the separator a split would post.

## InnoDB: an index latch with a third mode

InnoDB's source describes its tree as "an ordinary B-tree, not a B-link tree": pages have prev/next pointers for
scans, but no high keys, and no descent moves right. Instead a reader–writer lock on the whole index,
`index->lock`, sits in front of the page latches, with a third mode, **SX**. From `sync0rw.cc`:

| Held \ requested | S | SX | X |
|---|---|---|---|
| **S** | compatible | compatible | waits |
| **SX** | compatible | waits | waits |
| **X** | waits | waits | waits |

In MySQL 8.0's `btr_cur_search_to_nth_level`:

- **Searches** (`BTR_SEARCH_LEAF`) and **leaf-only changes** (`BTR_MODIFY_LEAF`) take `index->lock` in S, S-latch
  every non-leaf page on the path and keep them until the leaf is latched, then release them all.
- **Inserts are optimistic first.** `row_ins_clust_index_entry` tries `BTR_MODIFY_LEAF`; a row that needs a split
  gets `DB_FAIL` and is retried with `BTR_MODIFY_TREE`.
- **`BTR_MODIFY_TREE`** takes `index->lock` in **SX**, so other tree changes wait but searches continue. It
  buffer-fixes the path without latches — nothing else may restructure the tree — then latches only the pages the
  change can reach, top-down: the root in SX, since it holds the file-segment headers the new page comes from (X if
  the root itself changes), the parent in X, then the leaf's left sibling, the leaf and its right sibling in X,
  **left to right**.

Select **InnoDB index lock** and race it. The inserter restarts once on `DB_FAIL`, takes SX on `index->lock` and on R
while the reader holds S on both, then waits for P and L2. With the parent full it holds seven pages at once. Now tick
**MySQL 5.6 (X index lock)**: SX first appears in the 5.7 source, and 5.6 took `index->lock` in **X** for tree changes
while every descent merely buffer-fixed non-leaf pages. Step the inserter five times, then the reader: the reader
cannot even begin its search. SX turned an index-wide pause into waits on individual pages. The ordering that keeps all
of this deadlock-free is the index lock first, pages top-down, leaves left to right.

## Optimistic lock coupling

Shared latches cost something even when nobody waits: acquiring S **writes** to the latch to count readers, so the
root's latch word bounces between CPU caches although nobody modifies the root — the effect the
[buffer-pool latching page](../../../p02-storage-engines/buffer-pool-memory-mmap-and-contention/04-latching-the-buffer-pool-and-hot-page-contention/)
measures on hot pages. Crabbing, InnoDB and B-link readers all pay it at every level.

**Optimistic lock coupling** (OLC), as described by Leis, Haubenschild and Neumann, validates instead. Each node packs a
version counter and a lock bit into one 64-bit word. A reader reads the version (restarting if the lock bit is set),
reads the node, then re-reads the version and restarts if it changed. A writer locks by compare-and-swap from the
version it read to that version plus the lock bit, modifies, and unlocks by bumping the version. The coupling is the
order of checks: after reading a child pointer, the reader validates the node *before* dereferencing the pointer
(`checkOrRestart` in the paper), because a pointer read mid-modification may be garbage. Readers write nothing.

Select **Optimistic lock coupling** and race it. The reader records R v1, P v1 and L2 v1. The inserter write-locks P and
L2, splits L2, unlocks both at v2 and restarts, as the reference OLC B-tree does after every split. It locks only a node
and its parent, so it cannot carry a split upward; instead it splits any full inner node on the way down. The leaf split
filled P, so the second descent splits P (R v2, P v3) and restarts again before inserting 27 into L2′. The reader's
speculative search of L2 `[20, 25]` says "not found"; validation sees P at v3 and L2 at v2, discards that wrong answer,
restarts, and finds 30 in L2′. Reset, step the inserter four times and the reader twice: the reader restarts because P
is write-locked. OLC readers never wait; they retry, and the paper suggests capping retries and falling back to
pessimistic latching. Tick **Parent P is full too** and choose 12: the inserter splits P and restarts although L1 has
room, and the reader, whose parent P changed under it, restarts too.

In the paper's benchmark on a 10-core machine, OLC lookups at 20 threads ran 3.9× faster than traditional lock
coupling. LeanStore uses OLC, and Umbra adopts it as a **versioned latch**. Umbra's paper adds a shared mode, which it
says LeanStore lacked, so read-heavy scans stop failing validation; LeanStore's current `HybridLatch` has one too. Speculative code must terminate even on a torn node, and a
node cannot be freed while an optimistic reader may be inside it: the [next page](../02-memory-models-atomics-and-what-makes-optimistic-latching-cor/) covers why the
version check is sound on real hardware and how LeanStore and Umbra lay out their version words, and [memory reclamation](../04-memory-reclamation-for-latch-free-structures-epochs-hazard-p/)
covers the freeing.

## Lehman–Yao B-link trees

Lehman and Yao (1981) observed that if pages never merge, only one thing can happen to a child between releasing its
parent and latching it: **the child can split**, and a split only moves keys right. So every page gets a **right
link** to its sibling and a **high key**, an upper bound on its keys. A descent that finds its key **greater than the
high key** knows the page split after the downlink was read, and follows the right link, repeatedly if necessary.
A descent never needs to hold two latches, and a split becomes two independent steps — split the page, then post the downlink —
with a valid tree in between.

PostgreSQL's nbtree README describes the code as "a correct implementation of Lehman and Yao's high-concurrency
B-tree management algorithm", adapted to shared buffers:

- **A read lock per page.** Lehman and Yao assumed private copies of pages; PostgreSQL holds a shared content lock
  while examining a shared buffer.
- **One lock on the way down.** `_bt_search` will "drop the read lock on the page, then acquire one on its child";
  an insert write-locks only the leaf.
- **Move right.** `_bt_moveright` steps right while the scan key exceeds the high key at `P_HIKEY`, or while the page
  is half-dead or deleted.
- **Split.** `_bt_split` write-locks the left page, the new right page and the old right sibling (to fix its left
  link), and flags the left page `BTP_INCOMPLETE_SPLIT`.
- **Parent.** `_bt_insert_parent` re-finds the parent from the descent stack, write-locks it while still holding the
  left page, releases the right page, then inserts the downlink and clears the flag atomically.
- **Order.** A second page may be locked before releasing the current one only when moving right or up.

Select **B-link (PostgreSQL)** and race it. Between pages the reader holds no latch, and neither thread waits. At L2 the
reader sees 30 is above the high key 25 and makes one right-link hop to L2′. The inserter holds at most three pages;
with the parent full, four (L2, P, P′ and P's old right sibling Q). Untick **Reader checks the high key**: the same
schedule returns "not found" from L2 `[20, 25]`, the bug the high key exists to catch. Re-tick it, reset, and step the
inserter five times, then the reader five times: its read lock on L2 waits for the split. A B-link reader never holds
one page while waiting for another, but it still waits whenever the page it wants is write-locked.

If a crash interrupts a split between its two steps, the flag stays and the next insert passing through calls
`_bt_finish_split` — the [crash-safety page](../../../p02-storage-engines/b-trees-and-variants/03-crash-safety-of-structure-modifications-atomic-splits-and-in/)
shows that state. The OLC paper names the limit: releasing the parent early is a big win when the child must be read
from disk, but in memory a B-link descent still takes one latch per level. OLFIT (2001) added version counters to
B-link trees; OLC dropped the B-link part.

## Deleting pages under a B-link tree

Move-right works only because keys cross a page boundary in one direction, rightward in a split. Merging partly full
pages would move items across boundaries, and a scan travelling the other way could miss them, so nbtree **never
merges**. It deletes only completely empty pages, never the rightmost page of a level, in two stages from Lanin
and Shasha:

1. **Half-dead:** remove the downlink from the parent and set `BTP_HALF_DEAD`. The key range now belongs to the right
   sibling, and a search that still arrives moves right.
2. **Deleted:** lock the left sibling, the page and the right sibling, in that order; unlink it and set `BTP_DELETED`.

A backend may still be on its way to the page, so it stays as a tombstone with its sibling links intact — the README's
"drain technique". It is stamped with the next transaction ID, and VACUUM recycles it once that ID is visible to
everyone. Up to PostgreSQL 13 the stamp lived in `btpo.xact`, a union sharing space with the page's level; since 14 it
is a 64-bit `safexid` in `BTDeletedPageData`, checked by `BTPageIsRecyclable` via `GlobalVisCheckRemovableFullXid`, and
a VACUUM can recycle pages it deleted itself at the end of the same run. The stages are on the
[insert and delete page](../../../p02-storage-engines/b-trees-and-variants/02-insert-split-delete-and-merge/).

## The protocols side by side

| | Reader holds | Split holds | A concurrent split is caught by | Deadlock avoided by |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Latch crabbing | S on parent and child | X from the lowest safe ancestor down | not needed: parent stays latched | top-down order |
| InnoDB 5.7+ | S on `index->lock`, S on the path until the leaf | SX `index->lock`; root SX or X, parent X, three leaves X | not needed: SX excludes tree changes | index lock, top-down, left to right |
| OLC (LeanStore, Umbra) | nothing written; versions remembered | X on node and parent by compare-and-swap | changed version: restart | restarting instead of waiting |
| B-link (PostgreSQL) | one read lock | left, new right, old right sibling; then parent | key above high key: move right | second page only rightward or upward |

## Where it bites in production

- **Upper-level latch traffic.** Shared latches on hot internal pages become cache-line contention before anyone
  waits; queued writers show up in PostgreSQL as `LWLock` / `BufferContent`, and flat throughput with pinned CPUs is
  the shared-mode signature.
- **InnoDB serializes tree changes per index.** Constant splits, as with random UUID keys, queue on one index's SX
  latch, `wait/synch/sxlock/innodb/index_tree_rw_lock` in `performance_schema`. When the undo history list exceeds
  100,000 (`BTR_CUR_FINE_HISTORY_LENGTH`) and page reads are pending, delete-intended tree changes (mostly purge) take
  it in **X**, blocking searches too.
- **Restart storms.** Optimistic readers landing on pages under write keep restarting, long scans worst — hence
  retry caps and Umbra's shared mode.
- **Deleted index pages that cannot be reused.** A long-running transaction holds back the XID horizon, so pages VACUUM
  deleted stay unrecyclable and new splits extend the index instead.
- **`failed to re-find parent key in index`** is raised when `_bt_insert_parent` cannot find the downlink: corruption,
  not contention.

:::note[What to take forward]
A split moves keys right while other threads sit between a parent and a child. Crabbing never opens that gap, at the
price of X latches from the lowest safe node down (or a restart, if it descends optimistically); InnoDB adds an SX index
lock so tree changes exclude each other but not searches. Optimistic lock coupling makes readers write nothing and
restart when a version moves. Lehman–Yao lets the gap exist and gives every page a right link and high key to recover —
which is why PostgreSQL holds one read lock at a time, splits in two steps, and defers recycling deleted pages until no
snapshot can reach them.
:::
