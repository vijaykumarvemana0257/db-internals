A B-tree needs a total order in which nearby keys sit next to each other. Rectangles, polygons and time ranges have
no such order. Sort boxes by their left edge, and two boxes with the same left edge at opposite ends of the map
become neighbours. Sort bookings by start time, and a week-long booking lands far away from the short ones it
overlaps. The questions people ask — does this polygon intersect that one, which bookings overlap this
interval, what are the ten nearest shops — are not a range in any single ordering.

The **R-tree** (Guttman, SIGMOD 1984) keeps the B-tree's shape: balanced, page-sized nodes, growth at the root. It
replaces separator keys with **minimum bounding rectangles** (MBRs). Nearly everything else on this page follows from
one concession: sibling boxes are allowed to overlap.

## The invariant, and what overlap costs

A leaf entry is `(MBR, tuple id)`, where the box encloses the indexed object. An internal entry is `(MBR, child
pointer)`, where the box encloses every entry in the child page. Every page except the root holds between *m* and *M*
entries, with *m* ≤ *M*/2. The root has at least two children unless it is itself a leaf, and all leaves sit at the same
depth.

A window search starts at the root and follows **every** entry whose box intersects the window. A B-tree follows exactly
one child per level. An R-tree may follow zero, one or several, and nothing bounds the worst case below "every page".
That holds even for exact-match lookups.

The lab opens on 200 small boxes, pages of up to *M* = 10 entries and the R*-tree algorithm. The dashed query window
intersects 15 rectangles. The search reads **9 of the tree's 34 pages**: at 3 of the 4 internal pages it reads, more
than one child box intersects the window, so the search forks. One page, drawn dashed, is
read and matches nothing. Its box meets the window only in empty space.

Pages are read for nothing when the window hits **dead space** inside a box, and **overlap** multiplies the cost: a
window in a region two siblings share must enter both. Drag the window and watch the page count and the forks change.
Insertion decides how much dead space and overlap the index has, and a plain R-tree never revisits the decision.

## Insertion: choose a subtree, split when full

Guttman's insertion descends from the root. At each level it picks the entry whose box needs the **least area
enlargement** to cover the new rectangle, breaking ties by the smaller box. It places the entry in a leaf, then enlarges
the boxes along the path. A page that reaches *M* + 1 entries must **split** into two groups of at least *m* entries
each. Trying every partition is exponential in *M*, so Guttman proposed two approximations:

- **Quadratic split.** *PickSeeds* takes the pair that would waste the most area if boxed together:
  area(J) − area(E1) − area(E2), where J covers both. *PickNext* then repeatedly takes the remaining entry with the
  largest difference between the enlargement each group would need, and adds it to the group that grows less. Once a group needs every
  remaining entry to reach *m*, it gets them all.
- **Linear split.** For each axis, the seeds are the entry with the highest low side and the entry with the lowest high
  side. Their separation is divided by the width of the whole set, and the axis with the greatest normalized separation
  wins. The remaining entries are assigned in any order.

Set **Pointer** to **Draw** and move **Rectangles inserted** from 10 to 11. The eleventh rectangle overflows the only
page, the page splits, and a new root appears above the two halves. Under the default
R*-tree algorithm the halves hold 5 and 6 entries. Switch to **Quadratic** and the same insertion splits 7 + 4.

With all 200 rectangles in, open **Show the numbers**, which rebuilds the same data under each algorithm. Sibling-box overlap
comes to **62.6%** of the plane with the linear split, **16.5%** with quadratic and **7.8%** with the R*-tree. Across
100 windows of the current size, that averages 7.86, 7.23 and 6.58 pages per query.

## The R*-tree: optimize overlap and margin, then reinsert

Beckmann, Kriegel, Schneider and Seeger (SIGMOD 1990) tested combinations of area, **margin** (the sum of a box's edge
lengths) and overlap, and kept what performed best:

1. **ChooseSubtree.** At the level just above the leaves, pick the entry whose box gains the **least overlap** with its
   siblings; break ties by area enlargement, then by area. Higher up, keep Guttman's rule.
2. **Split.** For each axis, sort the entries by lower edge and, separately, by upper edge. Consider every cut that
   leaves at least *m* entries on each side (*M* − 2*m* + 2 per sort). Choose the axis whose cuts have the **smallest
   total margin**, which favours squarish boxes. On that axis, take the cut with the **least overlap**, with ties broken
   by area.
3. **Forced reinsert.** The first time a level overflows during one insertion (the root excepted), do not split. Remove
   the 30% of entries whose centres lie farthest from the page's centre and insert them again, nearest first. Split only
   if a page overflows again.

The paper found *m* = 40% of *M* best; the lab uses that minimum for all three algorithms.

Switch back to **R\*-tree** and untick **Forced reinsert**. Overlap drops from 7.8% to 6.7%: on random-order data at this size, reinsertion buys
nothing. Now choose the data set **inserted left to right**. Quadratic reaches 30.2% overlap and the R*-tree 9.3%; tick
**Forced reinsert** again and it falls to 6.7%. Reinsertion earns its cost when early boxes were placed before the rest of
the data existed. Finally, choose **Long thin strips**. Every algorithm leaves more than 160% overlap and reads about 9 to
14 pages per window, against 6 to 8 for small boxes. There the overlap comes from the data, and no split can remove it.

SQLite's R\*Tree module uses Guttman's ChooseLeaf with the R\*-tree split and *m* = *M*/3.
InnoDB's `SPATIAL` indexes split pages with Guttman's quadratic `pick_seeds`/`pick_next` (`gis0geo.cc`). PostgreSQL's
built-in box and point opclasses use area enlargement as the penalty and Korotkov's **double sorting split**, which, like
the R*-tree, looks for the cut along each axis that minimizes overlap.

## GiST: the R-tree with the geometry taken out

Hellerstein, Naughton and Pfeffer (VLDB 1995) noticed that B-trees and R-trees share a skeleton: balanced trees of pages
whose internal entries hold a **predicate** true of everything beneath them. The **Generalized Search Tree** keeps that
skeleton — page layout, splits, WAL logging, concurrency, the search loop — and asks an **operator class** for the
data-type logic:

| Method | Question it answers | R-tree version |
|---|---|---|
| `consistent` | Could anything under this entry satisfy the query? Sets `recheck` if the answer may be approximate | box intersects or contains the query |
| `union` | What key covers all these entries? | the MBR |
| `penalty` | How bad is adding this key to this subtree? | area enlargement |
| `picksplit` | How do the entries of a full page divide into two? | quadratic, R*-tree, double sorting |
| `same` | Are two keys equal? | box equality |
| `compress` / `decompress` | What is stored in the page instead of the value? | geometry → bounding box |
| `distance` | What is a lower bound on the distance to this entry? | distance to the box |

`fetch`, `options`, `sortsupport` and, in PostgreSQL 18, `translate_cmptype` complete the optional list. `consistent`, `union` and `same` decide correctness;
`penalty` and `picksplit` decide only speed.

The same skeleton runs very different indexes:

- **PostGIS geometry.** The leaf key is a `BOX2DF`: four `float4` values, rounded **outward** so the stored box never
  shrinks. `&&` compares boxes. `ST_Intersects` uses the index to find candidates and then tests the exact geometry. The
  penalty is area enlargement, or perimeter enlargement when the area does not change, and `picksplit` is the double
  sorting split.
- **Range types and exclusion constraints.** `range_ops` indexes intervals as one-dimensional boxes.
  `EXCLUDE USING gist (during WITH &&)` enforces "no two bookings overlap": each insert searches the index for a
  conflicting row and fails with *conflicting key value violates exclusion constraint*. Adding a scalar column such as
  `room WITH =` needs the `btree_gist` extension. PostgreSQL 18's `PRIMARY KEY (id, valid_at WITHOUT OVERLAPS)` builds
  on the same machinery.
- **Text search.** `tsvector_ops` hashes each word to one bit of a fixed signature — 124 bytes by default, configurable up
  to 2,024 with `siglen` — and ORs the bits together; `union` ORs signatures too. Two words can share a bit, so the index is **lossy** and every
  match is rechecked against the row. The documentation prefers [GIN](../06-gin-inverted-indexes-and-posting-lists/) for text search.

## Nearest neighbour: best-first over the same tree

`ORDER BY geom <-> $1 LIMIT 10` does not search a window. Since PostgreSQL 9.1, GiST answers it with a priority
queue — a pairing heap in current `gistget.c` — holding both pages and index tuples, keyed by distance. For a leaf tuple, `distance`
returns the distance to the query point. For an internal entry, it returns a lower bound: the smallest distance anything
beneath could have, usually the distance to the box. The scan pops the smallest item. A tuple is returned at once,
because nothing left in the queue can be closer. A page has its entries pushed. `LIMIT` stops the scan. This is Hjaltason and
Samet's best-first **distance browsing** (TODS 1999).

Choose **200 small boxes, random order** again, switch the lab to **KNN point** and press **Restart search**, then **Step**. The first three pops are pages at distance 0,
because the point lies inside their boxes. The fourth pop returns rectangle #88 at distance 3.0 as result 1 of 5. After three results, a page at distance 4.9 comes off the queue *before* the
fourth result at 5.2, since a box that close might still hold something nearer. The search finishes at 8.2 after expanding **9 of 34 pages**, with the queue
peaking at 46 entries. Set **k nearest** to 1 and only 3 pages are expanded. At 10, 11 are.

PostGIS's `<->` computes only the box distance and sets `recheck` on leaf
entries, so the executor computes exact distances from the heap rows and reorders them.

## Concurrency, crash safety and builds

A B-tree reader detects a concurrent split by comparing its search key with the page's **high key** and moving right, as
the [B-tree structure-modification page](../../../p02-storage-engines/b-trees-and-variants/03-crash-safety-of-structure-modifications-atomic-splits-and-in/)
showed. GiST keys have no order, so there is no high key to compare with. Kornacker, Mohan and Hellerstein (SIGMOD 1997)
use a **node sequence number** instead, and PostgreSQL implements it as a special-purpose LSN in each page header:

- A split links the left half to the new right page with a **right link** and marks the left half `F_FOLLOW_RIGHT` until
  the parent receives the new downlink. The downlink insertion then clears the flag and sets the child's **NSN** to the LSN
  of the parent update.
- A scan remembers the parent's LSN when it queues a child. If the child's NSN is greater, or the flag is still set, the
  child split after the parent was read, so the scan queues the right sibling too.
- Insertion widens downlink keys with `union` on the way **down**. The tree is therefore consistent the moment the leaf
  insert lands. A split interrupted by a crash leaves a flagged page, and the next insertion that meets it finishes the
  split.

One-at-a-time inserts turn into random I/O once the index outgrows the cache, so PostgreSQL has two other
build methods:

- The **buffered build** follows Arge, Hinrichs, Vahrenhold and Vitter. Internal pages carry buffers, and tuples flow
  down in batches. With the default `buffering = auto`, the build switches to this method once the index reaches
  `effective_cache_size`, and the buffers need temporary disk space up to the size of the index.
- The **sorted build** (PostgreSQL 14) is the default when every opclass supplies `sortsupport`. It sorts the tuples and packs
  leaves bottom-up, like a B-tree build. `point_ops` sorts by **Z-order** (Morton code) and PostGIS by **Hilbert** value
  of each box's centre. PostgreSQL 18 extended sorted builds to range types and `btree_gist`. Index quality then depends
  on how well the curve keeps neighbours together.

## The other way: turn space into a key

An R-tree **divides the objects**: page boxes follow wherever the data happens to be. The alternative **divides the
space**. Quarter the plane recursively and give each cell an id whose bits interleave x and y: that is a Z-order code, and
**geohash** writes it in base 32. A Hilbert ordering numbers the cells so that consecutive ids are always neighbouring cells.

A location becomes an ordinary key, and a window becomes a **covering**: a few cells, hence a few key-range scans on a
plain B-tree or LSM, whose candidates are then tested exactly. There are false positives, never false negatives.

- **MongoDB.** The `2d` index stores geohash values. `2dsphere` indexes S2 cell ids, with coverings bounded by a maximum
  cell count and finest and coarsest levels.
- **CockroachDB.** Spatial indexes use **S2**: a quadtree on each face of a cube around the sphere, numbered along a
  Hilbert curve. `s2_max_cells` defaults to 4 and `s2_max_level` to 30. Its documentation's reasons: it scales
  horizontally, needs no rebalancing, and inserts take no locks.
- **DynamoDB and Cassandra.** Neither has a spatial index, and Cassandra has no geometry type. AWS's Geo Library for DynamoDB keeps geohash keys computed with S2, runs several
  `Query` calls per search and filters the candidates itself, so every false positive costs read capacity.
- **H3.** Uber's grid uses hexagons instead of squares: 122 base cells, each finer resolution about seven times smaller in
  area, down to resolution 15.

| | R-tree / GiST | Cell ids on a B-tree or LSM |
|---|---|---|
| Structure follows | the data | a fixed grid |
| Large or long object | one entry with a big, overlapping box | many cells, or one coarse cell full of false positives |
| Window query | descends every intersecting subtree | a few range scans plus a filter |
| Insert | box enlargement up the path, page splits | one ordinary key insert per covering cell |

## Where it bites in production

- **Big, long or diagonal geometries.** Country outlines, rivers and road networks have boxes that are mostly empty space.
  `&&` then returns a flood of candidates, and the exact test on large polygons dominates the query. `ST_Subdivide` cuts
  them into pieces of at most 256 vertices by default, with far tighter boxes.
- **Recheck hides in the plan.** Lossy keys such as text signatures show up as `Rows Removed by Index Recheck` in
  `EXPLAIN ANALYZE`, for index scans and bitmap heap scans alike. PostGIS's exact test after `&&` counts instead as
  `Rows Removed by Filter`. Either way the rejected rows were read from the heap first, as with the lossy bitmaps on the
  [bitmap scan page](../03-bitmap-indexes-and-bitmap-heap-scans/).
- **The tree remembers its history.** Early boxes were placed before later data existed, so a long-lived index can be
  worse than a fresh build. Compare size and scan cost after [`REINDEX CONCURRENTLY`](../09-building-indexes-online-create-index-concurrently-internals/).
- **KNN needs the operator in `ORDER BY`.** Sorting by `ST_Distance(...)` or another function computes the distance for
  every row. Only an ordering operator such as `<->` lets the index return rows in distance order, and a `LIMIT` lets it stop early.
- **Signatures saturate.** Over long documents, a `tsvector` GiST index sets most of its 992 default signature bits, and
  internal pages, which OR many documents together, look consistent with nearly any query.

:::note[What to take forward]
An R-tree is a B-tree whose keys are bounding boxes that may overlap, so a search follows every intersecting subtree. The
split heuristic sets how much overlap and dead space every later query pays for: linear worst, quadratic better, the
R*-tree's overlap- and margin-aware split usually best. GiST keeps the balanced, logged, concurrent tree, with NSNs in place
of high keys, and lets an operator class supply `consistent`, `union`, `penalty` and `picksplit`; that one access method
serves PostGIS, range exclusion constraints, text signatures and KNN. Cell ids trade the adaptive tree for ordinary keys
that shard and bulk-load like any other, and pay in false positives.
:::
