A buffer pool that has been serving a 99% hit rate for weeks can be taken to 30% by a single
`SELECT count(*)` — not because the scan is slow, but because every page it reads looks, to a
recency-based replacement policy, exactly like the most valuable page in the pool. The scan
wins every eviction decision it participates in, and it participates in all of them. Every
serious engine therefore has a mechanism that lets a scan read a billion pages without being
allowed to keep any of them.

## Why LRU loses to a scan every time

Replacement policies rank pages by *recency*, because recency is the cheapest available proxy
for *reuse*. A sequential scan breaks the proxy. Each page it reads has a reuse distance of
infinity — the scan will never come back to it — while simultaneously having the best possible
recency score, because it was touched a microsecond ago. LRU cannot tell those two facts apart,
so it evicts pages that will be read again in milliseconds to make room for pages that will
never be read again at all.

The arithmetic is unforgiving. If the pool holds `P` frames and the scan reads `S > P` pages,
then by the end of the scan, under strict LRU, *every* frame holds a scan page: the scan has
issued more distinct references than the pool can hold, and LRU keeps the most recent `P` of
them. It does not matter how hot the evicted pages were or how long they had been resident.
This is the pathological case behind the k-competitive bound from
[the previous page](../../../p02-storage-engines/buffer-pool/04-how-good-can-a-cache-policy-be-opt-competitive-ratios-and-th/):
an adversary that wants to make LRU miss on everything writes a loop over `P + 1` pages, and a
sequential scan is that adversary by accident.

Run the lab with **Plain LRU** and drag through the scan window. The hot set is evicted from
the tail at exactly the rate the scan inserts at the head, the point-read hit rate falls to
roughly the fraction of the pool the hot set can still defend, and — the part people forget —
it stays down after the scan finishes. The pool is now full of pages that will never be read
again, and the only way out is to fault the working set back in one miss at a time. A five-second
scan can cost minutes of degraded latency.

The fix is not a cleverer general-purpose policy. It is to let the engine say, at the call site
that issues the read, *"this access has no reuse; do not let it compete."* That is what a
`BufferAccessStrategy`, a midpoint insertion point, and `fill_cache=false` all are.

## PostgreSQL: strategy rings, and the quarter-of-the-pool rule

PostgreSQL gives certain operations a **`BufferAccessStrategy`** — a small ring of buffer IDs
that the operation allocates from and recycles, instead of taking a fresh victim out of the
shared clock sweep each time. The ring is created in `freelist.c` by `GetAccessStrategy()`,
and there are three sizes:

| Strategy | Ring size | Used by |
|---|---|---|
| `BAS_BULKREAD` | 256 kB (32 buffers at the default `BLCKSZ`) | Sequential scans of large tables, the heap scan behind `CREATE INDEX` |
| `BAS_BULKWRITE` | 16 MB | `COPY FROM`, `CREATE TABLE AS`, `CREATE MATERIALIZED VIEW`, table-rewriting `ALTER TABLE` |
| `BAS_VACUUM` | 256 kB before PostgreSQL 16; since 16 it is `vacuum_buffer_usage_limit`, default **2 MB**, overridable per statement with `VACUUM (BUFFER_USAGE_LIMIT ...)` | `VACUUM`, autovacuum, `ANALYZE` |

Two rules decide whether a ring is used at all. First, the ring is capped so it can never be an
undue fraction of the pool — it is silently capped at an eighth of `shared_buffers`, which
matters on the tiny pools people give test instances. Second, and more importantly,
`initscan()` only turns the strategy on when the relation is **larger than a quarter of
`shared_buffers`** (`NBuffers / 4`). A scan of a small table is allowed to populate the pool
normally, because a small table genuinely might be worth caching; a scan of a big one is not,
because it cannot fit anyway.

The ring itself is a circular array of buffer IDs. When the scan needs a frame,
`GetBufferFromRing()` looks at the next slot and reuses that buffer **only if nobody else has
become interested in it** — the check is that its pin count is zero and its `usage_count` is at
most one. If another backend has pinned the page or bumped its usage count, the strategy gives
up on that slot, takes a fresh buffer from the normal clock sweep, and installs it in the ring.
That single condition is what keeps the mechanism safe: a page that turns out to be hot after
all gets promoted out of the ring's control rather than being recycled out from under its
readers.

Dirty pages are the other subtlety, and it is the reason the write ring is 64× the read ring.
Evicting a dirty buffer means writing it, and writing it means first flushing the WAL through
its page LSN — the rule [page 6](../../../p02-storage-engines/buffer-pool/06-dirty-pages-write-back-and-the-wal-before-data-rule/)
covers in full. With a 32-buffer ring, a bulk *write* would hit that cost every 32 pages, so
`COPY` would spend its life in `XLogFlush()`. `StrategyRejectBuffer()` handles the read side of
the same problem: if the buffer the ring offers back is dirty, a `BAS_BULKREAD` strategy rejects
it and falls back to the clock sweep, so a plain `SELECT` never gets stuck writing out somebody
else's dirty page to make room in its own ring. Bulk writes and vacuum do not reject — writing
is what they came to do — they just get a ring large enough to amortize the flush.

Note what the ring does *not* cover. It is attached to heap sequential scans, vacuum, analyze
and the bulk-write paths. An ordinary index scan, and the heap fetches of a bitmap heap scan,
allocate buffers the normal way. A query plan that reads half a large table through an index is
every bit as capable of flooding the pool as a sequential scan would have been, and it gets no
protection at all.

## Synchronized scans: many readers, one set of reads

The ring makes a second problem visible: if five reporting queries each scan the same 40 GB table
through their own 256 kB ring, the table is read from disk five times — and it would be read five
times without the ring too, since it does not fit in the pool either way. PostgreSQL's
answer is **synchronized sequential scans**, controlled by `synchronize_seqscans` (on by
default) and gated by the same size threshold.

The mechanism is a small shared hash table mapping a relation to the block number a scan of it
most recently reported. A starting scan looks the relation up and, instead of starting at block
0, starts *where the existing scan already is*. It reports its own position back every 128 kB
of progress. The scans then move through the file as a pack: the leader faults each page in,
the followers find it already present, and one physical read feeds all of them. On a table far
larger than memory this turns N× the I/O into 1×.

:::caution[The visible side effect]
A synchronized scan starts in the middle of the table and wraps around. So
`SELECT * FROM big_table LIMIT 10` with no `ORDER BY` can return *different rows on every
execution*, depending on where another scan happened to be. This is legal — SQL guarantees no
order without `ORDER BY` — but it surprises people, and it is why the behaviour has an off
switch.
:::

## InnoDB: the midpoint, and the clock that makes it work

InnoDB takes the other approach: one LRU list, but with two insertion points. The list is split
into a **new (young) sublist** at the head and an **old sublist** at the tail, with the boundary
— the midpoint — placed so the old sublist is about 3/8 of the list. That fraction is
`innodb_old_blocks_pct`, default **37**, adjustable from 5 to 95.

A page read from disk is not inserted at the head. It is inserted **at the midpoint**, at the
head of the old sublist. It is promoted to the head of the young sublist only when it is
accessed *again*. Since a scan of a table many times the pool's size touches each page once, a
scan can only ever churn through the old sublist — 3/8 of the pool — and the young sublist,
where the OLTP working set lives, never notices. Read-ahead pages enter at the midpoint too, so
speculative I/O that turns out to be wrong costs nothing but a slot in the old sublist.

That design has a hole, and the hole is why the second knob exists. A "single touch" per page is
a lie at the row level: a heap page holds dozens of rows, and a scan reads all of them, back to
back, in microseconds. Under a naive "promote on second access" rule the scan's own second row
would promote the page into the young sublist and the flood would be back. So InnoDB gates the
promotion on *time*: **`innodb_old_blocks_time`**, default **1000** milliseconds, is the window
a page must survive in the old sublist before an access is allowed to promote it. Row two of a
scanned page arrives a microsecond after row one, so it does not qualify. A genuinely hot page
read now and read again a second later does.

Set the lab to **Midpoint (InnoDB)** and flip `innodb_old_blocks_time` between 1000 ms and 0. At
1000 ms the scan's pages pile up below the midpoint and evict each other; the young sublist
holds still. At 0 the scan's second touch of each page promotes it, the young sublist is
overwritten from the head, and the midpoint strategy collapses into plain LRU — the same curve
as the first tab. One knob, two completely different outcomes, and it is the knob most likely to
have been "tuned" to zero by somebody chasing a microbenchmark.

InnoDB reports on this directly. `SHOW ENGINE INNODB STATUS` has, in the BUFFER POOL AND MEMORY
section, `Database pages`, `Old database pages`, `Pages made young`, `not young`, and the rates
`youngs/s` and `non-youngs/s`. A scan that is being correctly resisted shows a high
`non-youngs/s` and a near-flat `youngs/s`: pages are being read and refused promotion, which is
exactly what you want. A spike in `youngs/s` during a reporting query is the signature of
midpoint insertion being defeated. `information_schema.INNODB_BUFFER_PAGE_LRU` gives the
per-page view, at a cost high enough that you should not query it on a busy production server.

There is one more piece of relink avoidance worth knowing about, because it is a contention
mechanism rather than a scan-resistance one: a hit on a page that is *already* near the head of
the young sublist does not move it. Relinking costs the LRU list mutex, and a page two positions
from the head gains nothing by becoming position zero. InnoDB uses an approximate age counter to
decide, so the hottest pages in the system stop paying list maintenance on every access.

## RocksDB, Oracle, and the option of not caching at all

RocksDB's block cache has both defenses, expressed as options rather than heuristics:

- **`ReadOptions::fill_cache = false`** tells an iterator not to insert the data blocks it reads
  into the block cache at all. Compaction reads use it — compaction is the archetypal
  zero-reuse scan, and inserting its blocks would evict the live read path's working set — and
  you can set it on any analytical iterator you write.
- **Priority pools.** `Cache::Insert()` takes a `Cache::Priority`, and the LRU cache keeps a
  high-priority pool whose size is set by `high_pri_pool_ratio`. Low-priority entries are
  inserted at the midpoint of the list, exactly as InnoDB does, and are promoted into the
  high-priority pool on a subsequent hit. With
  `cache_index_and_filter_blocks_with_high_priority`, index and filter blocks — the ones whose
  eviction costs an extra I/O on *every* subsequent lookup — go straight into the high-priority
  pool, where a data-block scan cannot reach them.

Oracle skips the pool entirely: a full table scan of a sufficiently large segment does a
**serial direct path read**, streaming blocks into the session's PGA instead of the buffer
cache, with the size decision keyed off `_small_table_threshold` (a small fraction of the
cache). The buffer cache is not merely protected, it is not involved. The same is true of
parallel query, which has always used direct path.

And some engines have no defense. SQLite's page cache is a plain LRU with a `PRAGMA cache_size`
bound; a scan of a large table will evict everything, which matters less than it sounds because
SQLite usually sits on top of an OS page cache doing its own thing.

## The price of the defense: a bypassed scan cannot warm the pool

Every one of these mechanisms trades away the one good thing a scan does — it brings pages into
memory. Three consequences follow, and all three show up in real systems.

**The medium-sized table that is never cached.** PostgreSQL applies the ring to any table larger
than `shared_buffers / 4`. With `shared_buffers = 64GB`, a 20 GB table gets a 256 kB ring — even
though it would fit in the pool four times over with room to spare, and even if it is scanned
every minute. Each scan reads it from storage again. The fixes are blunt: raise `shared_buffers`
until the table is under the threshold, or force the pages in with `pg_prewarm()` and rely on the
fact that a page already resident is found by the scan and never enters the ring at all. The
Linux page cache often saves you here — the read is a page-cache hit rather than a device read —
which is one of the few good arguments for PostgreSQL's double-buffered design.

**The cold start after a bulk load.** `COPY` into a new table uses `BAS_BULKWRITE`. When it
finishes, the table is on disk and almost none of it is in the pool, so the first queries against
freshly loaded data are all misses. Loading then measuring makes the load look fine and the query
look terrible.

**Vacuum starving itself.** A vacuum of a large, heavily dirtied table recycles a small ring —
256 kB before PostgreSQL 16, 2 MB by default since — and each recycled dirty buffer needs a WAL
flush before it can be written. That is deliberate
throttling — it is why vacuum does not obliterate the pool — but on a big maintenance window it
is also why vacuum can be much slower than the hardware allows. PostgreSQL 16's
`VACUUM (BUFFER_USAGE_LIMIT '256MB')` exists precisely so you can trade scan resistance for
speed when nobody is watching, and `BUFFER_USAGE_LIMIT 0` removes the ring entirely.

## Where it bites in production

- **The nightly report that breaks the morning.** The symptom is a p99 latency step on the OLTP
  path that outlasts the analytical query by many minutes, with CPU flat and device reads up. In
  PostgreSQL, `pg_stat_database.blks_read` climbing while `blks_hit` stalls is the signal; the
  proof is `pg_buffercache`, grouping resident buffers by relation and finding the reporting
  table holding most of the pool. If it is holding the pool despite the ring, the plan was an
  index or bitmap heap scan, which no strategy covers.
- **`innodb_old_blocks_time = 0` in the config.** Someone set it years ago, and from then on
  every full scan promotes its pages. `youngs/s` spiking in `SHOW ENGINE INNODB STATUS` during
  a batch job is the tell. The MySQL-recommended countermeasure to a known scan window is the
  opposite move: raise `innodb_old_blocks_time` (to several seconds) or drop
  `innodb_old_blocks_pct` (to 5) for its duration, both of which are dynamic.
- **A backup that evicts the working set.** `mysqldump` does full scans; `pg_dump` does
  sequential scans and therefore gets the ring, but a logical backup driven by an index-ordered
  cursor does not. Physical backups (`pg_basebackup`, Percona XtraBackup) read files outside
  the engine entirely and touch neither pool — but they do evict the OS page cache, which on a
  PostgreSQL host is the second-level cache.
- **Replica promotion into a cold pool.** Scan resistance means a standby that has been
  replaying WAL is warm in exactly the pages WAL touched and cold everywhere else, so the first
  analytical query after failover is also the first scan. That is the warm-up problem from
  [page 2](../../../p02-storage-engines/buffer-pool/02-cache-warm-up-cold-pools-dump-load-and-prewarming/),
  and prewarming is the answer to it — not the ring.
- **Benchmarks that measure the wrong pool.** A `SELECT count(*)` run twice on a table above
  the ring threshold is slow both times, and it is not a caching bug. A benchmark that reports
  "PostgreSQL does not cache my table" has usually just found `NBuffers / 4`.

:::note[What to take forward]
Recency is a proxy for reuse, and a sequential scan is the case where the proxy inverts: maximum
recency, zero reuse. No general-purpose replacement policy can fix that from the inside, so
engines fix it from the call site — PostgreSQL hands scans, vacuum and bulk writes a small
recycled ring of frames; InnoDB inserts every newly read page at the midpoint and refuses to
promote it until it has survived `innodb_old_blocks_time`; RocksDB lets the reader say
`fill_cache=false` and keeps index and filter blocks in a high-priority pool. All of them work,
and all of them cost the same thing: a scan that cannot flood the pool also cannot warm it.
:::
