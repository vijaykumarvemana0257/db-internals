Every miss in the buffer pool forces a decision the engine cannot take back: which resident page
loses its frame. That decision is made millions of times a second, on the same data structure every
backend is touching, and it is wrong in two directions at once — a policy that is too dumb evicts
the page you are about to read, and a policy that is too clever spends more on bookkeeping per hit
than it saves in avoided I/O. Every real replacement policy is a point on that trade.

Set the loop longer than the frame count and step through it: under LRU the hot pages are evicted by
loop pages that will never be read again before they too are evicted, and the hot-set hit rate
collapses. Switch to ARC on the same trace and watch the hot pages sit in T2 while the loop churns
through T1.

## The cost of being exactly right

Textbook LRU is a doubly linked list of frames ordered by last touch. A hit unlinks the entry and
relinks it at the head; a miss takes the tail. Both are O(1), which makes the algorithm look free —
and it is not, for three reasons that only show up at database scale.

First, **the work is on the hit path**. A healthy pool runs at a 99% hit rate, so the list splice is
paid 99 times for every eviction it informs. Second, the splice writes to *shared* memory — pointer
stores into nodes other cores are reading — so a cache line bounces between sockets on every buffer
access. Third, the list needs a lock, and it is one lock for the whole pool; a per-frame latch does
not help, because the mutation spans three nodes. The list move is the only reason that lock exists.
(What the contention does to a many-core machine belongs to a later module; here the point is just
that exact recency has a per-hit price.)

Engines that keep an LRU list therefore cheat on precision to buy back the hit path. InnoDB keeps a
real LRU list, but skips the move-to-head when the block is already near the head: the eviction order
would not change, so the list operation and its lock are simply not taken.

## CLOCK: one bit instead of one list

CLOCK keeps the frames in a fixed circular array — the same array of frames and descriptors the pool
already has, described in [buffer pool structure](/p02-storage-engines/buffer-pool/01-buffer-pool-structure-frames-page-table-and-pins/) — plus a
**reference bit** per frame and a **hand** that points at the next candidate. A hit sets the bit and
does nothing else: no list, no shared ordering, no global lock. On a miss the hand advances; if the
frame under it has its bit set, the bit is cleared (a second chance) and the hand moves on; the first
frame found with a clear bit is the victim.

The invariant is worth stating precisely, because it is what makes the approximation acceptable:
**CLOCK never evicts a page that has been referenced since the hand last passed it.** Within that
resolution the victim is an old page; it just is not guaranteed to be *the* oldest.

PostgreSQL runs a generalized version — a counter instead of a bit, which makes it frequency-aware,
not just recency-aware. Each buffer descriptor carries a `usage_count` inside its atomic state word.
Pinning a buffer increments it, capped at `BM_MAX_USAGE_COUNT`, which is **5**; a newly read page
starts at 1. `StrategyGetBuffer` takes a frame from the freelist if one is there, and otherwise
sweeps: at each frame, if `usage_count > 0` it decrements and advances, and the first frame with
`usage_count == 0` and no pins becomes the victim. The hit path is a compare-and-swap on the buffer's
own state word, not a mutation of a structure shared with every other buffer.

Two consequences follow directly from the cap and the decrement:

- A page must be passed over **at most five times** before it becomes evictable, so the sweep is
  bounded — but in the worst case, where every frame is at 5, a single allocation walks up to five
  full revolutions of the pool. A 32 GB `shared_buffers` is four million 8 KB frames, so that worst
  case is on the order of twenty million descriptor visits for one page read. Watch the "frames per
  sweep" statistic in the lab climb as you shrink the pool relative to the hot set.
- Because the counter saturates at 5, CLOCK cannot distinguish a page read 6 times from one read
  6000 times. The frequency signal is deliberately coarse; it exists to protect the hot set from a
  single pass, not to rank it.

You can see the counter distribution directly:

```sql
SELECT usagecount, count(*), count(*) FILTER (WHERE isdirty) AS dirty
FROM pg_buffercache GROUP BY usagecount ORDER BY usagecount;
```

A pool where almost every buffer sits at 5 is a pool whose sweep is doing five passes of work per
allocation, and it is telling you the working set no longer fits.

## The flaw both of them share

LRU and CLOCK rank pages by *when*, never by *how often*. A page read once by a `COPY` and a page
read on every index descent enter the pool at the same priority, and the one-touch page is by
definition the more recent. That is the whole weakness, and it shows up as two named pathologies:

- **Sequential flooding** — one large scan evicts the entire working set, because every scanned page
  is newer than everything it displaces. The engine-level defenses are their own subject; see
  [scan resistance](/p02-storage-engines/buffer-pool/05-scan-resistance-sequential-flooding-ring-buffers-and-midpoin/).
- **The looping cliff** — a cyclic access pattern over *N* pages in a pool of *N−1* frames. Under
  pure recency the next page needed is always the page just evicted, and the hit rate is **exactly
  zero**. Not degraded: zero; one more frame takes it to 100%. Set the loop one page longer than the
  frame count in the lab. That discontinuity is why buffer pool capacity planning is not a linear
  extrapolation.

The policies that follow all answer the same question: how do you recognize a second reference
cheaply enough to act on it?

## LRU-K: measure the K-th reference back

O'Neil, O'Neil and Weikum's LRU-K (SIGMOD 1993) replaces "time of last reference" with the
**backward K-distance** b(p, K): how far back you have to look to find the K-th most recent reference
to page p. Evict the page with the largest backward K-distance; a page referenced fewer than K times
has distance infinity and goes first. With K = 1 this is exactly LRU. With **K = 2** — which the paper
argues captures nearly all the available benefit — a page touched once is instantly distinguishable
from a page touched twice, no matter how recently the single touch happened.

Two details make it work in practice, and both are in the paper:

- **The retained information period.** History must outlive residency. If you forget a page's
  reference times the moment you evict it, you can never observe its second reference, and LRU-2
  degenerates to LRU. So the algorithm keeps reference history for pages that are no longer in the
  pool — the `history` row in the lab under the LRU-K policy — for some period longer than a typical
  reuse interval.
- **The correlated reference period.** A page touched five times inside one transaction is not five
  times hotter than one touched once; those references are *correlated*. LRU-K collapses references
  that arrive within a short window into a single one, so a burst cannot fake a hot page. (The lab
  omits this window; real implementations must not.)

LRU-K is the reference point for "does this policy know about frequency", and it is essentially never
implemented in a buffer pool hot path. Choosing a victim means finding a maximum over all resident
pages — a priority queue with an O(log n) update on every single access — and you must maintain
history for pages you are not even caching. That is the exact cost profile the hit path cannot
afford. Its value is as a specification: the policies below are constant-time approximations of it.

## 2Q: LRU-K's answer in O(1)

Johnson and Shasha's 2Q (VLDB 1994) gets the LRU-2 effect out of three simple queues:

| Structure | Contents | Discipline | Default size |
|---|---|---|---|
| `A1in` | pages seen once, resident | FIFO | Kin ≈ 25% of the pool |
| `A1out` | *identifiers only* of pages evicted from `A1in`, not resident | FIFO | Kout ≈ 50% of the pool |
| `Am` | pages seen at least twice, resident | LRU | the remainder |

A page nobody remembers enters `A1in` and is never promoted by a hit there — a second touch while the
page is still in the FIFO is treated as a correlated reference, exactly the LRU-K idea. It ages out of
`A1in`, drops its frame, and leaves its *identifier* in `A1out`. Only when the page is requested again
while its id is still in `A1out` — proof of a genuinely independent second reference, with the first
one far enough back to have fallen out of the FIFO — does it enter `Am`. One-touch pages therefore
live and die in a quarter of the pool and never displace anything hot.

`A1out` is the crucial half: it is a **ghost list**, holding page identifiers (in Postgres terms, a
`BufferTag` — relation, fork, block) and no page data. Remembering 50% more pages than you cache costs
a few tens of bytes each rather than 8 KB each.

This is, in slightly different clothing, what MySQL ships. InnoDB splits its single LRU list into a
**young sublist** and an **old sublist**, inserts every newly read page at the *midpoint* between
them rather than at the head, and promotes a page into the young sublist only on a *later* access —
`A1in` and `Am` with one list and a pointer instead of two lists. It even has the correlated-reference
window, expressed in the time domain by `innodb_old_blocks_time`: a page re-read too soon after
entering the old sublist is not promoted, which is exactly the "a scan touches every row on the page
it just read" case. The knobs, their defaults and the monitoring belong to
[scan resistance](/p02-storage-engines/buffer-pool/05-scan-resistance-sequential-flooding-ring-buffers-and-midpoin/).

## ARC: let the workload set the knob

Megiddo and Modha's ARC (FAST 2003) keeps the same idea but removes the tuning. The pool of c frames
is split between two resident lists, each with its own ghost list:

- **T1** — pages referenced exactly once recently (recency). Ghost: **B1**.
- **T2** — pages referenced at least twice (frequency). Ghost: **B2**.

The invariants: `|T1| + |T2| ≤ c` (they share the pool), `|T1| + |B1| ≤ c`, and the four lists
together hold at most 2c entries. A hit in T1 promotes the page to T2. A miss on a page nobody
remembers enters T1.

The adaptation is the whole trick, and it is one number, `p`, the target size of T1. A hit in **B1**
means "I evicted a recency page and immediately needed it again", so `p` increases and T1 grows; a hit
in **B2** means a frequently-used page was dropped too soon, so `p` decreases and T2 grows. The ghost
lists are how the policy learns from mistakes it has already made: every ghost hit is a correction
applied to `p`, so the split follows the workload as it changes shape. There is no knob for an
operator to get wrong, every operation is constant time, and the self-tuning costs only the ghost
entries. In the lab, watch `p`
move in the narration line as you shift the trace between loop-dominated and hot-set-dominated.

ARC's absence from open-source relational engines is a legal story rather than a technical one: IBM
holds a patent on it. PostgreSQL shipped ARC in 8.0 and ripped it out in a *minor* release — 8.0.2,
April 2005 — when the patent surfaced, substituting a 2Q variant; the buffer manager rewrite in 8.1
later replaced that with the clock sweep and `usage_count` that Postgres still runs today. Where you
do meet ARC in production is ZFS, whose adaptive cache is a modified ARC — the same MRU/MFU lists and
ghost lists, extended to handle buffers that cannot be evicted on demand.

## LIRS, CLOCK-Pro, TinyLFU, and OPT

Two more families are worth knowing by name. **LIRS** (Jiang and Zhang, SIGMETRICS 2002) ranks pages
by *reuse distance* — distinct pages touched between two consecutive references to the same page —
rather than by recency, which handles the looping cliff in a way LRU-K and ARC only approximate;
H2's MVStore ships a LIRS cache, and **CLOCK-Pro** does for LIRS what CLOCK does for LRU. **TinyLFU**
and its windowed variant W-TinyLFU instead keep an approximate frequency sketch (a count-min sketch
with periodic halving) over far more keys than fit in the cache, and admit a new page only if the
sketch says it is hotter than the page it would evict — the design behind Caffeine.

Above all of them sits **OPT** — Belady's MIN, 1966 — which evicts the resident page whose next
reference is furthest in the future. It needs the future, so it cannot be implemented, but it can be
computed from a trace afterwards, which makes it the only honest yardstick: the OPT curve in the lab
is the hit rate no online policy can beat on that trace with those frames. Note that OPT minimizes
*total* misses and will sacrifice a hot page to save two others, so its hot-set hit rate is sometimes
lower than ARC's. How close an online policy can get in the worst case is
[the next page](/p02-storage-engines/buffer-pool/04-how-good-can-a-cache-policy-be-opt-competitive-ratios-and-th/).

## Where it bites in production

- **The hit rate is fine and the latency is not.** In PostgreSQL, a pool whose `pg_buffercache`
  `usagecount` histogram is piled up at 5 makes every allocation walk a long sweep while holding
  buffer mapping locks. The symptom is `BufferMapping` wait events and rising `buffers_alloc` in
  `pg_stat_bgwriter` with no change in query plans; `pg_stat_io` breaks the same story down into
  `evictions` and `reuses`.
- **A nightly job that wrecks the morning.** A scan, a `pg_dump` or a backup reads the whole table,
  and under a recency policy the working set is gone by the time traffic arrives; the pool then
  spends hours re-reading it, with the same cold-pool arithmetic as
  [cache warm-up](/p02-storage-engines/buffer-pool/02-cache-warm-up-cold-pools-dump-load-and-prewarming/).
- **A burst of references is not frequency.** Every frequency-aware policy on this page can be
  fooled by correlated references: a scan that touches all ~200 tuples on one heap page, or a nested
  loop that re-probes the same index leaf a thousand times inside one statement, looks like a very hot
  page to a naive counter. That is what LRU-K's correlated-reference period, 2Q's refusal to promote
  on an `A1in` hit and InnoDB's `innodb_old_blocks_time` all exist to suppress, and it is why
  PostgreSQL's `usage_count` saturates at 5 rather than counting freely. A policy that skips this
  step evicts the real working set in favour of whatever the last big statement walked over.
- **The cliff, in a graph.** A workload whose loop just outgrew the pool shows a hit rate that falls
  much faster than the data grew, and no policy on this page repairs it. More memory does.

:::caution[Do not benchmark a policy on a random trace]
Uniform-random access makes every policy here identical: no page is hotter than another and no
reference predicts the next, so there is nothing to learn. Policies separate only on traces with
structure — a skewed hot set, a loop, a scan mixed into an OLTP workload. If ARC and LRU land within
noise of each other, check the trace before concluding the policy does not matter.
:::

:::note[What to take forward]
Exact LRU is rejected for its cost on the *hit* path, not for its accuracy, and CLOCK buys that cost
back by replacing an ordered list with one counter per frame — PostgreSQL's `usage_count`, capped at
5 and decremented by the sweep. Everything past CLOCK is an attempt to separate a page touched once
from a page touched repeatedly: LRU-K measures the K-th reference back and is too expensive to ship,
while 2Q and ARC get most of the effect in constant time by remembering the *identifiers* of pages
they have already evicted. And no policy rescues a working set that has outgrown the pool: the
looping cliff goes from 100% to 0% for one page of growth.
:::
