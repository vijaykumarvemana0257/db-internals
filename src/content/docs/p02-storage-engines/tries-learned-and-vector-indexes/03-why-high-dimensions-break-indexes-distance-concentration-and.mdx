A tree index answers "what is nearest to this point?" with a proof: it keeps the best answers so far and skips every
region that cannot contain anything closer. In two or three dimensions that discards almost the whole tree. In a few dozen
dimensions of unstructured data it discards nothing, because nearly every point is about the same distance from the query,
and the index becomes a sequential scan with extra pointer chasing.

Which of those two cases you are in does not depend on the length of your vectors. It depends on the dimension of the data itself.

## Distances concentrate

Draw a query and a point uniformly from the unit cube in *D* dimensions. Their squared distance is a sum of *D*
independent terms, each with mean 1/6 and variance 7/180. The sum's mean grows like *D* but its standard deviation
only like √*D*, so the relative spread of the distance shrinks like 1/√*D* — roughly 0.59/√*D* for the cube. At 256
dimensions the standard deviation of the distance is under 4% of its mean.

Beyer, Goldstein, Ramakrishnan and Shaft turned this into a theorem in *When Is "Nearest Neighbor" Meaningful?* (ICDT
1999). Fix the number of points. If the variance of the ratio of distance to expected distance goes to zero as dimension
grows, then for every ε > 0 the probability that the farthest point is within (1 + ε) of the nearest goes to one. That
condition is much broader than independent, identically distributed coordinates. Their experiments on real and synthetic
data found the effect appearing at as few as 10–15 dimensions.

The lab starts on the uniform cube at *D* = 4. The histogram pools every query-to-point distance over 40 queries, each
divided by that query's mean distance. The dashed and solid lines mark the average positions of the 1st and 10th
neighbours. Move **Ambient dimension D**:

- At *D* = 2 the farthest point is on average about 107 times as far as the nearest. The histogram is wide and the
  neighbours sit near zero.
- At *D* = 8 the ratio is 5.30, at *D* = 64 it is 1.60, and at *D* = 256 it is 1.26. The histogram collapses into a spike
  around 1, and the 10th neighbour sits at 0.91 of the mean distance.

The **Relative contrast** tile is He, Kumar and Chang's difficulty measure (ICML 2012): mean distance divided by nearest
distance. It falls from 39.27 at *D* = 2 to 1.13 at *D* = 256. Near 1, the "nearest" answer is barely closer than a random point.

## Why partitioning indexes turn into scans

Every space- or data-partitioning index prunes with the same test. A branch — a kd-tree cell, an R-tree bounding
rectangle, an M-tree ball, a quadtree square — has a lower bound on the distance from the query to anything inside it. If
that bound is at least the current *k*-th best distance, the branch is skipped. So pruning only works while the *k*-th
best distance is small compared with the distance to most cells.

Concentration removes exactly that. The lab's **kd-tree** splits the coordinate with the largest spread at its median and
stops at 12 points per leaf. It searches best-bin-first using the exact distance from the query to each cell's box, so
with no budget it is an exact 10-nearest-neighbour search, and the grid shows which of its 128 leaves the first query had
to open. On the uniform cube, an exact search opens about 4 leaves on average at *D* = 2 (3.2% of the points), 11.5 at *D* = 4,
and 62 at *D* = 8, about half the data. At *D* = 16 it averages 127.98, and from *D* = 32 up every query opens every leaf.

Weber, Schek and Blott found a second, structural cause in their VLDB 1998 analysis. A tree over *N* points with *m* points
per leaf has about log₂(*N*/*m*) levels, so each leaf cell is bounded on at most that many coordinates and spans the full range
of all the others. The lab's tree has seven levels: at *D* = 64 each leaf is cut on at most 7 of the 64 coordinates. The ball
around the query out to its 10th neighbour reaches into nearly every cell. For rectangular bounding regions (R\*-tree,
X-tree, SR-tree) on uniform data, Weber and colleagues calculated that a search reads more than 20% of the blocks beyond roughly
15, 18 and 20 dimensions for 10⁵, 10⁶ and 10⁷ points, and that partitioning methods in general tend towards O(*N*): a
sequential scan beat them on average above about 10 dimensions. The crossover grows with log *N*, which is why the lab's 1,500
points collapse so early.

This is why scikit-learn's documentation calls its `KDTree` very fast below about 20 dimensions and inefficient as
dimension grows, and why `algorithm='auto'` switches to brute force whenever *D* > 15. It also explains PostgreSQL's
`cube` extension. Its GiST operator class uses Guttman's R-tree split and can serve `ORDER BY c <-> cube(...) LIMIT k`, but it caps
cubes at 100 dimensions, and its [bounding boxes](../../../p02-storage-engines/indexes-beyond-the-b-tree/05-gist-and-r-trees-for-spatial-and-range-data/)
hit the same wall well before that on unstructured data. The same geometry applies to the
[quadtrees and k-d trees behind SP-GiST](../01-sp-gist-tries-and-adaptive-radix-trees/).

## Intrinsic dimension is what counts

The theorem has a condition, and real data often violates it. Beyer et al. describe two ways out: clustered data where
the query falls inside a cluster, and data whose underlying dimensionality is much lower than its number of coordinates.
Levina and Bickel (NIPS 2004) open by citing the consensus that methods work in very high dimensions only because the data are not truly high-dimensional.

Switch **Data** to **Curved m-dim sheet**. Each point starts as *m* latent coordinates, bent into 2*m* coordinates, then rotated
into *D* dimensions by a fixed random rotation. A rotation preserves distances, so moving *D* from 8 to 256 draws a fresh sample with the same
geometry. At *m* = 4 the relative contrast stays between 6.08 and 6.72 and the intrinsic-dimension estimate stays at
3.9–4.1. The histogram never collapses.

Now select **Hyperplane tree**. It splits each node the way Spotify's Annoy does: seed two centres with two
sampled points, move them with a weighted 2-means over sampled points, and cut on the hyperplane equidistant between them.
Those cuts follow the data wherever it lies, and an exact search reads 16–18% of the points at every *D* from 8 to 256. Switch
back to **kd-tree**: it reads 20% at *D* = 8, 46% at *D* = 32, 76% at *D* = 64, and opens 127 of its 128 leaves on average at *D* = 256.

The sheet did not change. The kd-tree cuts one coordinate at a time, and after a rotation every coordinate mixes all the latent ones. Dasgupta and Freund (STOC 2008) showed that k-d trees do not adapt to intrinsic dimension — points on the coordinate axes,
intrinsic dimension log₂ *D*, need *D* levels to halve a cell's diameter — and proved that splitting along random directions does adapt.
A partitioning index can count on low intrinsic dimension only if its cuts are not tied to the coordinate axes.

Low is relative, though. Raise **Intrinsic dimension m** to 8. The estimate reads 7.1–7.6 across *D* and relative contrast drops to
2.7–2.9. At *D* = 16, exact search already reads 92% of the points in the kd-tree and 94% in the hyperplane tree. Real corpora are
higher still. Aumüller and Ceccarello estimated local intrinsic dimension for standard benchmark sets (SISAP 2019): medians of 13.2
for MNIST's 784 pixels, 19.2 for 128-dimensional SIFT, 17.8 for 100-dimensional GloVe and 23.4 for 300-dimensional GloVe-2M. That is
far easier than random vectors of the same length, yet too high-dimensional for exact pruning — the regime approximate indexes exist for.

### Estimating it

The lab's estimate is Levina and Bickel's maximum-likelihood estimator, in the per-point form Aumüller and Ceccarello use. For a
point whose nearest-neighbour distances are r₁ ≤ … ≤ r_k:

```
LID = −( (1/k) · Σᵢ ln(rᵢ / r_k) )⁻¹
```

The lab uses *k* = 20 and reports the median over queries. The answer depends on scale: Levina and Bickel note that a line with noise added can look one- or two-dimensional depending
on the neighbourhood size. And every estimator they compared is biased low at higher dimensions. On the uniform cube the lab reads
35.7 at *D* = 64 and 78.6 at *D* = 256, so treat a large estimate as a lower bound.

## LSH: hashing instead of partitioning

Locality-sensitive hashing gives up on exact pruning and bounds the cost with probability instead. A hash family is
**(r, cr, p₁, p₂)-sensitive** if points within distance *r* collide with probability at least *p₁* and points beyond *cr* collide
with probability at most *p₂* < *p₁*. The E2LSH family for Euclidean distance, from Datar, Immorlica, Indyk and Mirrokni, projects
onto a random Gaussian direction and cuts the line into buckets of width *w*: h(v) = ⌊(a·v + b)/w⌋, with *b* uniform in [0, *w*].
Its collision probability depends only on distance ÷ *w*, and the E2LSH implementation uses *w* = 4 with the radius *r* scaled to 1.

A single hash separates near from far poorly, so it is amplified twice:

- **AND.** Concatenate *k* hashes into one bucket key. A pair lands together with probability *p*ᵏ, which drives far points down
  faster than near ones.
- **OR.** Build *L* independent tables and take every point that shares the query's bucket in any of them. A pair becomes a
  candidate with probability 1 − (1 − *p*ᵏ)ᴸ.

That curve has an S shape. *Mining of Massive Datasets* describes the same construction for MinHash as *b* bands of *r* rows, with
the threshold near (1/*b*)^(1/*r*). The framework from Indyk and Motwani (STOC 1998) turns it into a bound: with ρ = ln(1/*p₁*)/ln(1/*p₂*), about
*n*^ρ tables answer a *c*-approximate query in roughly *n*^ρ time. For the p-stable family ρ < 1/*c*.

The lab draws the S-curve over the histogram, with *w* set to 4 × the mean 10th-neighbour distance. Weighting the
histogram by the curve gives the expected number of distinct candidates; the E2LSH manual's cost model, *L* × Σ *p*ᵏ(‖q − v‖),
counts a point once for every table it collides in.

- At *D* = 4 with *k* = 6 and *L* = 10, the curve is high over the neighbours and near zero over the bulk: 208 candidates out of
  1,500 (the **LSH candidates** tile).
- Set **LSH hashes per table k** to 16 and only 8 candidates remain — too strict to keep the neighbours. Raise **LSH tables L**
  to 40 and 21 candidates recover most of them.
- Return to *k* = 6 and *L* = 10 and move to *D* = 64. The spike now sits under the top of the curve, and 1,159 points are
  candidates. Any *k* steep enough to cut the spike cuts the neighbours too: at *k* = 16 and *L* = 40 there are still 552.

The **LSH exponent ρ** tile uses the 10th-neighbour distance for *r* and the mean distance for *cr*. It rises from 0.23 at *D* = 4 to
0.80 at *D* = 64 and 0.90 at *D* = 256, so query cost approaches *n*. He, Kumar and Chang prove the general form: the number of
candidates LSH must return grows as *n* raised to a function that shrinks as relative contrast grows.

## Graphs versus hashes on the same queries

The bottom chart gives every index the same budget — at most **Distance budget** percent of the 1,500 distances per query — and plots
recall@10, the fraction of the true 10 nearest neighbours returned, at each ambient dimension. Only distances to data points are charged: LSH's *k* × *L* projections and the hyperplane tree's
split tests come free, which flatters both. The dashed line is what a random sample of that many points finds: no index at all. The HNSW here is a small build (M = 8, `efConstruction` = 32); the
[vector index page](../04-vector-indexes-ivf-hnsw-and-quantization/) explains how it works.

On the uniform cube at 5%, all four methods score above 0.9 at *D* = 4, then fall together to between 0.09 and 0.12 at *D* = 256,
against 0.05 for the random line. Raise the budget to 20%. At *D* = 16, HNSW now finds 0.97 of the true neighbours while both trees and
LSH find about 0.74, and at *D* = 256 HNSW's 0.52 still leads. On the *m* = 8 sheet at *D* = 256 and 20%, HNSW reaches 1.00.

Published benchmarks on real vectors show the same order. Malkov and Yashunin compared HNSW with FALCONN (an LSH library), Annoy,
FLANN and a VP-tree on SIFT, GloVe, DEEP and CoPhIR, and HNSW won by a large margin. In ANN-Benchmarks, graph-based methods were the
fastest on both GloVe and SIFT. Synthetic data misleads in both directions: ANN-Benchmarks also built a set where most points have no structure but
each query's neighbours are well separated, and there no HNSW setting passed recall 0.86 while Annoy was fastest.

LSH still wins where its model fits the problem:

- **Near-duplicate detection.** Beyer et al. note that approximate-match queries stay meaningful when the query really lies within a
  small distance of a data point. Duplicates sit near distance zero, far left of the bulk, where the S-curve separates cleanly. Manku, Jain and Das Sarma (WWW 2007) found 64-bit Charikar simhash fingerprints with at most 3 differing bits
  suitable for an 8-billion-page crawl. Lee et al.'s 2021 deduplication of language-model training data used MinHash over 5-grams: a
  9,000-hash signature in 450 bands of 20, with candidates then kept only if their edit similarity exceeded 0.8.
- **Streams.** Inserting an item costs *k* × *L* hash computations and *L* bucket appends. Nothing is re-linked, so there is none of
  the [maintenance a graph index needs under churn](../05-how-churn-degrades-a-vector-index-tombstones-merges-and-cent/).

## Measure your corpus first

Before choosing an index, run the lab's measurements on your own data; they need one brute-force pass over a sample.

1. **Use queries that look like production.** Beyer et al. show the query distribution matters as much as the data: queries that
   fall inside clusters get meaningful answers, while queries between clusters face the same concentration when picking a cluster.
2. **Compute exact k-NN** for a few thousand of those queries against a sample of the corpus.
3. **Plot distance ÷ mean distance** and mark the 10th neighbour. A wide histogram with the neighbours well to the left gives
   partitioning, graphs and LSH something to work with. A spike near 1 means a partitioning index will scan, and every approximate
   index will pay heavily for recall.
4. **Record relative contrast and the LID distribution, including its tail.** Aumüller and Ceccarello found that query sets with
   higher LID needed more time to reach the same recall, and that LID has a long tail. Those tail queries become your recall misses
   and your slow queries.
5. **Allow for sample size.** Relative contrast rises with the number of points, so a small sample overstates difficulty.

## Where it bites in production

- **Benchmarks on random vectors.** Uniform random vectors are far harder than embeddings of the same length. Benchmark on real
  vectors and real queries.
- **Tree indexes on embeddings.** A GiST, R-tree or kd-tree index over dense embeddings, which spread information across every
  coordinate, builds and returns correct answers while doing the work of a full scan: query time grows with table size even though the plan says index scan.
- **The hard-query tail.** Mean recall hides high-LID queries. Break recall down by query difficulty.
- **Intrinsic dimension drift.** A new embedding model or content type can raise LID without changing the vector length, making
  every index more expensive at once. Recompute the histogram and LID estimate on a schedule.

:::note[What to take forward]
When distances concentrate, the *k*-th neighbour is nearly as far away as everything else, so no lower bound can exclude a cell:
partitioning indexes become scans and LSH's exponent ρ approaches 1. Intrinsic dimension controls concentration, not vector length,
though a kd-tree's axis-aligned cuts cannot exploit a low one. Benchmark corpora measure in the teens and low twenties, far easier than
random vectors but too hard for exact pruning, so graph indexes lead there while LSH keeps near-duplicate detection and streaming inserts.
Measure the distance histogram and LID of your own queries before you choose.
:::
